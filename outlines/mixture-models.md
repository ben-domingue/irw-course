# Mixture models: when respondents differ in kind (`mixture-models`)

Module: beyond · Prereqs: guessing-priors, person-fit · Extension · Status: stub (outlined)

#189 proposed `rasch`, `fit-prediction`, `guessing-priors`; the first two are ancestors of `guessing-priors`, so the list reduces to it. `person-fit` (outlined in parallel; prereqs `rasch`, `fit-prediction`) adds no new ancestors and makes its thread and table legal here (Open questions). `cdm` and `careless-responding` are not prerequisites; each gets an "if you've done" Recall (E2).

## Core ideas

1. **One population, or several?** *(major)* Each respondent belongs to one of $K$ latent classes (share $\pi_k$), and each class has its own measurement model. A pattern's likelihood is a weighted sum over classes, and Bayes' rule gives each respondent's posterior class probabilities. In the **mixture Rasch model** each class has its own difficulties $b_{ik}$ ([Rost, 1990](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662169001400305); [von Davier & Rost, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781315374512), *Handbook of IRT* Vol. 1, pp. 393–406). Within a class the sum score is still sufficient for $\theta$, but the *pattern* carries the class (thread `sum-score-sufficiency`). The safety valve of `guessing-priors` is the case where one class's model is fixed at chance ([Xiao, Ulitzsch, Zhang, Frank & Domingue, 2026](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/7gtwd_v1)). Pointer to `cdm` (E2): there classes are fixed by attributes; here they are defined by *which model a respondent follows*.
2. **Levels or kinds? Look for crossings.** *(major)* Classes that differ only in location are a continuum cut into pieces (thread `classes-vs-continuum`, from `constructs`). Classes differ in kind when their difficulties cross: specific objectivity holds within each class and fails between them (thread `specific-objectivity`). Classes can be solution strategies ([Mislevy & Verhelst, 1990](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf02295283)), as Siegler's balance-scale rules are ([Siegler, 1976](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(76)90016-5); [Jansen & van der Maas, 1997](https://doi.org/10.1006/drev.1997.0437)). The caution runs the other way too: a non-normal continuum or the wrong item model can produce classes that aren't kinds of people ([Bauer & Curran, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989X.8.3.338); [Lubke & Muthén, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989X.10.1.21)). A class needs outside evidence before it gets a name.
3. **Inattentive responding as a class that follows another model.** *(major)* The engaged class follows the Rasch model on keyed items. The inattentive class answers without regard to content, at chance or agreeing with everything; on a balanced scale that makes forward items look easy and reversed items hard ([Jin, Chen & Wang, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1094428117725792); [Ulitzsch, Yildirim-Erbasli, Gorgun & Bulut, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/bmsp.12272); [van Laar & Braeken, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/jedm.12317)). The comparison is with screening one respondent at a time ([Meade & Craig, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028085); E2 Recall of `careless-responding`) and with person fit (thread `misfit-many-causes`: a misfitting pattern says the model doesn't fit the respondent; a mixture proposes the model that does). On an all-forward scale, agreeing with everything looks like a high trait, so balanced keying is what makes the class visible.
4. **When responses can't tell, times can.** If the inattentive pattern is one the engaged model also produces (every item wrong on a test with no chance floor), responses can't separate the classes, and response times can inform membership ([Wang & Xu, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/bmsp.12054); [Ulitzsch, Pohl, Khorramdel, Kroehne & von Davier, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-021-09817-7)). `response-time` isn't an ancestor, so the lesson restates what it needs (E2).
5. **How many classes, and can we trust the fit?** *(major)* Another class always raises the likelihood. Choose $K$ with BIC ([Li, Cohen, Kim & Cho, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621608326422); [Nylund, Asparouhov & Muthén, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705510701575396)) and with held-out responses (thread `imv`; [Domingue et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-024-09977-2)). Shown, not asserted: **label switching** (classes are named by profile, not number; [Stephens, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9868.00265)) and **local maxima** (several random starts, `mirt`'s `nruns`; [Chalmers, 2012](https://doi.org/10.18637/jss.v048.i06)).
6. **Latent DIF (short).** Items can function differently between classes no observed variable picks out ([Cohen & Bolt, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2005.00007); this DOI resolves without the usual `.x`). One paragraph and problem 5; "if you've done `dif`" (E2).

**Tools (checked).** `mirt::multipleGroup(..., dentype = "mixture-K", nruns = )` fits mixture Rasch, 2PL and GRM models, and `START`/`FIXED` syntax fixes a class. Fixing one class at $P = 0.5$ reproduces the hand-written EM of `guessing-priors` exactly, in about 30 s (sanity row). `psychomix` ([Frick, Strobl, Leisch & Zeileis, 2012](https://doi.org/10.18637/jss.v048.i07); CRAN 1.1-10) fits Rasch mixtures but wasn't installed or tried, and isn't checked in webR: *Going further* only. Neither package fits responses and times jointly; idea 4 uses a short EM extending `guessing-priors`' `fit_mixture()`.

**Verdict (proposed; soft and impersonal).** In the case that a survey is balanced or a test has a known chance rate, a two-class mixture fitted beside the one-class model, and checked against something known (instructed-response items, times, where the sample came from), may be sufficient for some purposes as a first look at inattentive responding. The class is a hypothesis about how some respondents answered, not a verdict on who they are.

**The finding.** On a balanced scale, half the respondents follow a model in which content doesn't matter, and the model finds them without being told who they are: almost none from the panels that screen workers, most from MTurk, and many who passed every attention check. Their sum scores match everyone else's; only the pattern shows them. On the matrix test, responses alone just relabel the zeros, and times are needed to tell a fast zero from a slow one.

**Keying and data notes.** Kay: `resp` is the raw −3…+3 rating (OSF survey PDF). `_x`/`_r` marks the pair member, not the trait's direction (`cntr_005_x` is "I keep in the background"), so items are keyed from their wording. `blue` and `gibb` aren't antonym pairs; `pets` and `sick` are weak ones. Script `data/kay_2025_antonyms.py` keeps `finished == 1`; no waves. ## Widget / simulation / problem ideas

**Widgets**
- *Whose pattern is it?* (idea 1). Click a pattern on ten balanced items; see its likelihood under the engaged and content-free classes, the shares and the posterior. Two patterns with the same sum get very different posteriors.
- *Levels or kinds?* (idea 2). Two classes' profiles on the five balance item types; slide from "shifted" (stacked) to "Rule I vs. Rule II" (crossing). A toggle draws $\theta$ from a skewed distribution, and a two-class fit finds a "class" anyway (precomputed, seeded).
- *Balanced or all-forward?* (idea 3). An agree-with-everything class of chosen size; toggle the keying; watch the antonym correlation, the sum-score histogram and the class profile. All-forward, the class sits at the top of the trait.
- *Fast zero, slow zero* (idea 4). An all-wrong respondent; slide their mean time; posterior of the disengaged class from responses alone (flat) and with times.
- *How many classes?* (idea 5). BIC and held-out IMV against $K$ for simulated data; a rerun button that swaps labels and sometimes stops at a lower maximum.

**Predict-then-check:** "Among respondents who passed all four instructed-response items, what share does the mixture put in the content-free class: almost none, about one in ten, or more than a third?" Answer: 44% (54% on MTurk, 2% on Connect/Prolific), printed by the cross-tabulation.

**Simulate:** 1,000 respondents, ten balanced items; 80% Rasch, 20% agree with any item with probability 0.8. Fit Rasch and a two-class mixture in `mirt` (`nruns = 3`); compare $\hat\pi$, profiles and assignments with the truth; change the seed and watch the labels swap; fit $K = 3$ and read BIC. Then set the agreement probability to 0.5 (random responding, harder to find) or key everything forward. Check the browser time at drafting.

**Problems**
1. *Derivation.* The posterior class probability under a two-class mixture Rasch model; show that within a class it depends on $\theta$ only through the sum, and that the class posterior depends on the pattern.
2. *Real data with a twist.* Refit the Kay mixture on the neuroticism pairs (`mood`, `upst`, `rlxd`) and cross-tabulate the assignments. Is the content-free class a property of the respondent or of the scale?
3. *Judgment.* A colleague drops everyone in the content-free class before correlating extraversion with political orientation (`cov_poli_cont`). What changes, and what about posteriors near 0.5?
4. *Design.* A 20-item survey on which a mixture can find inattentive respondents: keying, antonym pairs, instructed items, timing. What does each buy?
5. *Real data: latent DIF.* Which Kay items function differently between classes, and does the class line up with the platform (Cohen & Bolt, 2005)? When is a class an observed group under another name?
6. *Challenge (open).* Fit the mixture GRM to the 7-point Kay ratings (preliminary: BIC 39,110 → 36,979 with two classes; one class answers every pair alike) and compare with the dichotomous fit. Does the gain survive against a skewed continuum? I don't know of a general test that tells a real class from a non-normal continuum.

## Go deeper

- **Why the pattern, not the sum, carries the class.** Under the mixture Rasch model $\Pr(\mathbf{x} \mid r, k) = \exp(-\sum_i x_i b_{ik})/\gamma_r(\mathbf{b}_k)$, so the class posterior depends on the pattern through each class's conditional likelihood and on the sum through the class's score distribution (Rost's 1990 conditional formulation). Why: extends `sum-score-sufficiency`; the basis of problem 1. Half a page.

What this is for

A fractions program for struggling fifth graders raises its students’ test scores by more than a standard deviation. Did it raise every item, or a handful? Differential item functioning ended on a vocabulary trial in which one word, expedition, carried over a quarter of the raw effect. This lesson takes that finding further. Experience, whether a program, the loss of a spouse or a second sitting of the same test, can change how a measure works, and a randomized trial is the one setting where we can separate that change from a change in the respondents. We read treatment as a grouping variable, use the pretest as a placebo, fit a model that gives every item its own treatment effect, and ask what the spread of those effects does to the average effect we report.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • say how experience can change a measure (alpha, beta and gamma change; response shift) as well as respondents’ standing on it;
  • use randomization to read treatment DIF as caused by the treatment, and a pretest as a placebo check on the whole analysis;
  • write down and fit the item-level heterogeneous treatment effects (IL-HTE) model with lme4, and interpret \(\sigma_\zeta\) and \(\rho\);
  • explain what item-level effects do to the precision and generalizability of an average effect, and how they can produce a treatment-by-pretest interaction that no respondent has;
  • decide, for a given use of the scores, whether the items that move most are a threat or the signal.

Core ideas

When experience changes the measure

After a spouse dies, older adults in the Health and Retirement Study report more depressive symptoms on an eight-item, yes/no version of the CES-D (Radloff, 1977), and the rise fades within about a year (Domingue, Duncan, Harrati & Belsky, 2021). The items don’t share the rise equally: loneliness and sadness carry most of it (the preprint, Domingue et al., 2019, Supplemental Figures S7–S8). Is that more depression, or depression measured differently? A widow who now reports feeling lonely may be no more depressed, in the sense the other items capture, than a year earlier; the loneliness item is also answering a question about her circumstances. The IRW has no item-level data from this study, so the result stays a cited figure.

The possibility has names. Golembiewski, Billingsley and Yeager (1976) distinguished three kinds of change. Alpha change is a change in level on an instrument that stays the same; beta change, a change in the scale respondents use to answer; gamma change, a redefinition of the construct itself (Livingston et al., 2022, give a short account). In health research, Sprangers and Schwartz (1999) call the last two response shift: a change in what a self-evaluation means, from new internal standards, new values or a new conception of the construct. Each is a failure of invariance across occasions. Gamma change is a change in the factor structure; beta change, unequal loadings or intercepts (Jabrayilov, Emons, de Jong & Sijtsma, 2017; Oort, 2005; Widaman, Ferrer & Conger, 2010).

Recall from Fairness in testing: impact is a difference in the attribute itself; bias is a difference in scores that comes from something else. Invariance is always relative to the other items: a shift shared by every item looks like impact.

Swap “group” for “occasion” and the fairness lesson carries over: a change in level is impact across time, and an item whose meaning shifts is DIF across time. A program, a bereavement and a second sitting are all experiences between two measurements, and each can change the measure along with the person.

If you’ve done Measurement invariance: longitudinal invariance is the multigroup ladder (configural, metric, scalar) with occasions in place of groups. Generalizability theory treats the occasion as a facet, a source of error to average over; here it is a condition that may change what is measured. Factor analysis I used BFI-2 data in which students were randomly assigned a response format, the same design with the format as the treatment. And What does a score mean? computed a reliable change index, which assumes the scale measures the same thing at both waves (Jacobson & Truax, 1991).

Treatment as the grouping, and the pretest as a placebo

Recall from Differential item functioning: in a randomized trial, treatment assignment is a grouping too. Among children with the same total, the vocabulary item expedition was far easier for treated children, and DIF by treatment is variation in the treatment effect from item to item.

After a bereavement we can’t separate change in the person from change in the measure, since who is bereaved isn’t random. A randomized trial removes the problem. At the pretest, treated and control respondents come from the same population, so there is no impact and, since the program hasn’t happened yet, no reason for any item to work differently. Any treatment DIF found then is a false positive, and the pretest becomes a placebo test for the whole analysis, from the handling of waves to the matching score. At the posttest the arms differ only by assignment, so an item that works differently for treated respondents does so because of the treatment (Gilbert, Kim & Miratrix, 2023; Ahmed et al., 2025).

What the treatment causes is still relative. If the program raised every item by the same amount, the matched comparison would find nothing: that is impact, the average effect. Treatment DIF is each item’s departure from the average.

A simulated trial: respondents in each arm answer the same Rasch items twice. At the pretest nobody has been treated. At the posttest everyone has grown by 0.5 logits, treated respondents by a further 0.5 on average, and each item’s effect departs from that average with the spread you set. Each dot is an item’s Mantel–Haenszel delta by treatment (positive: easier for treated respondents with the same total), orange when \(p < .05\).

A treatment effect for every item

Rather than test items one at a time, we can model the variation. The IL-HTE model (Gilbert, Kim & Miratrix, 2023; Gilbert, Himmelsbach, Soland, Joshi & Domingue, 2025) is the Rasch model with persons and items both random (De Boeck & Wilson, 2004) and a random item slope on treatment, \(T_j\): \[\begin{aligned} \text{logit}\Pr(x_{ij} = 1) &= \theta_j - b_i + \zeta_i T_j,\\ \theta_j &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 T_j + \varepsilon_j, \qquad \varepsilon_j \sim N(0, \sigma_\theta^2),\\ \begin{pmatrix} b_i \\ \zeta_i \end{pmatrix} &\sim N\!\left(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_b^2 & \rho\sigma_b\sigma_\zeta \\ \rho\sigma_b\sigma_\zeta & \sigma_\zeta^2 \end{pmatrix}\right). \end{aligned}\] \(\beta_1\) is the average effect on \(\theta\); item \(i\)’s effect is \(\beta_1 + \zeta_i\). \(\sigma_\zeta\) is the spread of item effects around the average. At zero every item moves by \(\beta_1\), which is what a Rasch model fitted to both arms assumes: treatment changes \(\theta\) and nothing else. \(\rho\) says whether harder items (positive) or easier items (negative) move more. Dividing \(\beta_1\) and \(\sigma_\zeta\) by \(\sigma_\theta\) puts them in standard deviations of ability.

It is a generalized linear mixed model, so lme4 fits it (Bates, Mächler, Bolker & Walker, 2015; De Boeck et al., 2011):

glmer(resp ~ treat + (1 | id) + (treat | item), data, family = binomial)

lme4’s item intercept is an easiness, \(-b_i\), so the correlation it prints is \(-\rho\). Is \(\sigma_\zeta\) zero? Compare this model with one that has (1 | item) by a likelihood ratio. A variance can’t be negative, so the null value sits on the boundary of the parameter space, and halving the \(\chi^2\) \(p\)-value is the usual correction (Self & Liang, 1987).

What item-level effects do to the estimate

Does the spread change the average? Not by itself: the sum score, a constant-effect model and the IL-HTE model estimate much the same average, as the fractions trial below shows. It changes three other things.

Precision. The items are a sample from those that could have been written, and if they respond unequally, a different sample would give a different average. Treating the items as fixed leaves that variation out, which understates standard errors and inflates false positives (Gilbert, Kim & Miratrix, 2023). Across 15 trials, Ahmed et al. (2025) found one item driving nearly 40% of an effect, and item-level patterns that could make 31% of the significant effects null once counted as uncertainty.

Generalizability. \(\sigma_\zeta\) says how far the effect on a fresh set of items from the same domain could fall from the one observed. A claim about the construct, rather than about these items, needs it (Gilbert, Himmelsbach, et al., 2025).

Recall from Validity as an argument: an interpretation/use argument has a generalization link, from the observed score to the score over all the tasks it stands for. \(\sigma_\zeta\) is evidence on that link for an effect rather than a score.

Interactions. Trialists ask who benefits most by interacting treatment with a pretest. Suppose every respondent gains the same amount on \(\theta\), but easier items respond more. In the treated arm the items’ effective difficulties, \(b_i - \zeta_i\), are more spread out than in the control arm, the sum score rises less steeply with \(\theta\), and a sum-score regression finds a negative treatment-by-pretest interaction that no respondent has. From sum scores alone the two stories can’t be told apart; item-level data can tell them apart (Gilbert, Miratrix, Joshi & Domingue, 2025).

NoteGo deeper: why the interaction appears

Assumptions: the IL-HTE model above; no person-level heterogeneity (every respondent’s \(\theta\) rises by \(\beta_1\)); the outcome analysed is the sum score \(S = \sum_i x_i\).

Two tests in one. In the control arm item \(i\) has difficulty \(b_i\). In the treated arm it behaves as if its difficulty were \(b_i - \zeta_i\), on top of the shift of \(\beta_1\) in \(\theta\). The spread of effective difficulties in the treated arm is \[\text{Var}(b_i - \zeta_i) = \sigma_b^2 + \sigma_\zeta^2 - 2\rho\sigma_b\sigma_\zeta,\] which exceeds \(\sigma_b^2\) whenever \(\rho \le 0\), and can fall below it when \(\rho\) is positive and large enough.

What spread does to the sum score. The expected sum score is the test characteristic curve, \(E(S \mid \theta) = \sum_i P_i(\theta)\) with \(P_i(\theta) = \text{logit}^{-1}(\theta - b_i)\). Its slope at \(\theta\) is \(\sum_i P_i(1 - P_i)\), the test information under the Rasch model. An item near \(\theta\) contributes close to the maximum of 1/4; spreading the items out moves some of them where they contribute little. So in the middle of the ability range the treated arm’s curve is flatter when \(\rho < 0\).

The interaction. A regression of \(S\) on treatment, pretest and their product estimates the slope of \(S\) on the pretest in each arm. The pretest predicts \(\theta\) equally well in both arms, but the treated arm’s flatter curve turns a unit of \(\theta\) into fewer points, so its slope is smaller and the interaction is negative, although every respondent gained the same. Gilbert, Miratrix, Joshi and Domingue (2025) show analytically and by simulation that a sum score cannot distinguish this from a true interaction, and that a model with item-specific effects can. The bounded range of \(S\) adds a smaller effect of its own, which is why the widget’s interaction is not quite zero when \(\sigma_\zeta = 0\).

Bias, or what the program taught?

Is an item that moves most a biased item? It may be the item closest to what an intervention taught. For evaluating the intervention, that can be the signal: the program changed what it set out to change, and the near items show it. For a claim of general gain, it is a threat: a test made mostly of near items overstates transfer, in the sense of Barnett and Ceci’s (2002) taxonomy of near and far transfer. Whether item-level heterogeneity is bias depends on the interpretation and the use, as Validity as an argument would lead you to expect. Group-specific item parameters can also be judged by prediction: do they predict new responses better than shared ones? The InterModel Vigorish measures that (Domingue et al., 2024). Explaining which items move, with features such as alignment to the curriculum, is a job for What is an item?.

Simulate

The code simulates a trial with 800 respondents and 20 items. Treatment raises every respondent’s \(\theta\) by 0.5, whatever their pretest; the items’ departures have \(\sigma_\zeta = 0.8\) and \(\rho = -0.8\) (easier items move more). It fits a sum-score regression, a constant-effect model and the IL-HTE model, each with treatment, pretest and their product. Loading lme4 takes 10 to 20 seconds the first time the page runs code; the fits take a few seconds more.

With the seed as written, the IL-HTE model recovers \(\sigma_\zeta\) = 0.78 and \(\rho\) = −0.78, and its item effects correlate 0.99 with the true ones. The interaction, 0 in truth, is −0.08 SD in the sum-score regression and −0.10 logits in the constant-effect model; the IL-HTE model puts it at 0.04. Its standard error is about 0.06 in both models, so a single run is noisy: try other seeds. Set rho <- 0.8 and the constant-effect interaction turns positive (0.03); set sigma_zeta <- 0 and the three models agree.

On waves: wave 0 is the pretest and wave 1 the posttest. The posttest repeats all 37 pretest items (26 from the fourth-grade Test for Understanding of Fractions, tuf4, and 11 fractions procedures) and adds 56: the fifth-grade form (tuf5), more procedures, and a block the dataset labels TransMath (trans_math). Eighteen posttest responses are missing, all from one student, who drops out of the complete-case analyses.

We match on the total score and test uniform DIF, the kind a shift in one item’s treatment effect produces, with the logistic regression of the DIF lesson (Swaminathan & Rogers, 1990). With 37 or 93 tests, we also control the false discovery rate (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995), and give the \(\Delta R^2\) categories of Jodoin and Gierl (2001).

Code 
# One wide matrix per wave: a row per student, a column per item (1 = correct).
wide <- function(long, items) {
  x <- long[long$item %in% items, ]
  A <- tapply(x$resp, list(x$id, x$item), function(v) v[1])
  A <- A[complete.cases(A), items]
  list(X = A, treat = kids20$treat[match(rownames(A), kids20$id)])
}
# Logistic-regression DIF for one item, matching on the total score, as in the DIF
# lesson but for uniform DIF only, the kind a shift in one item's treatment effect
# produces: the 1-df likelihood-ratio test of M0 (total) against M1 (total and
# treatment), and the change in Nagelkerke's R^2 between them.
nagelkerke <- function(m, ll0, n) {
  cs <- 1 - exp(2 * (ll0 - as.numeric(logLik(m))) / n)
  cs / (1 - exp(2 * ll0 / n))
}
lr_item <- function(y, g, total) {
  d <- data.frame(y = y, g = g, total = total)
  ll0 <- as.numeric(logLik(glm(y ~ 1, binomial, d)))
  m0 <- glm(y ~ total, binomial, d)
  m1 <- glm(y ~ total + g, binomial, d)
  c(beta_treat = unname(coef(m1)["g"]),
    p = anova(m0, m1, test = "LRT")[2, "Pr(>Chi)"],
    dR2 = nagelkerke(m1, ll0, nrow(d)) - nagelkerke(m0, ll0, nrow(d)))
}
# Every item; p-values also after the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate
# correction; Jodoin-Gierl categories (A below .035, B to .07, C above, and A
# whenever the test is not significant).
treatment_dif <- function(W) {
  tot <- rowSums(W$X)
  out <- as.data.frame(t(sapply(colnames(W$X), function(i) lr_item(W$X[, i], W$treat, tot))))
  out$p_BH <- p.adjust(out$p, "BH")
  out$jg <- ifelse(out$p >= 0.05 | out$dR2 < 0.035, "A", ifelse(out$dR2 < 0.07, "B", "C"))
  out
}

The IL-HTE model summarizes all of this in a few numbers:

Code 
suppressPackageStartupMessages(library(lme4))
# The IL-HTE model (Gilbert, Kim & Miratrix, 2023), in lme4's parameterization:
#   logit P(x_ij = 1) = beta0 + beta1 T_j + theta_j + e_i + zeta_i T_j,
# with theta_j ~ N(0, s_theta^2) and (e_i, zeta_i) bivariate normal. lme4's item
# intercept e_i is an easiness, the negative of the lesson's difficulty b_i, so the
# correlation it reports has the opposite sign to rho in the lesson; we flip it.
# Following the IRW vignette, effects are standardized by s_theta from the model with
# a constant effect, and sigma_zeta is tested by a likelihood-ratio test against
# that model, halving the p-value because a variance of zero sits on the boundary
# (Self & Liang, 1987).
ilhte <- function(long) {
  m0 <- glmer(resp ~ treat + (1 | id) + (1 | item), long, family = binomial)
  m1 <- glmer(resp ~ treat + (1 | id) + (treat | item), long, family = binomial)
  s_theta <- sqrt(VarCorr(m0)$id[1])
  vc <- VarCorr(m1)$item
  lr <- as.numeric(2 * (logLik(m1) - logLik(m0)))
  list(m0 = m0, m1 = m1, s_theta = s_theta,
       summary = c(students = length(unique(long$id)), items = length(unique(long$item)),
                   effect_constant_SD = unname(fixef(m0)["treat"]) / s_theta,
                   effect_average_SD = unname(fixef(m1)["treat"]) / s_theta,
                   sigma_zeta_SD = sqrt(vc[2, 2]) / s_theta,
                   rho_difficulty = -attr(vc, "correlation")[1, 2],
                   LR = lr, p = 0.5 * pchisq(lr, 2, lower.tail = FALSE)))
}

Does IL-HTE change the answer to “who gained most?” here? Barely: the treatment-by-baseline interaction is 0.024 logits with a constant effect for every item and 0.020 with IL-HTE, both well inside their standard errors. With a spread of 0.28 SD, against 0.70 in the fractions trial, and a moderate \(\rho\), there is little for the constant-effect model to misread.

What should a trialist take from this? Two positions rest on different assumptions. If the outcome’s items are the target (the state test a program will be judged on), the average over these items is the estimand, and the sum score answers it. If the items stand for a domain (fractions, health knowledge), the effect on the domain is the estimand, and the spread of item effects is part of its uncertainty. My practice, when a trial’s outcome comes with item-level data, is to fit the IL-HTE model beside the sum-score analysis, report \(\sigma_\zeta\) next to the average effect, and use the pretest as a placebo when there is one. In the fractions trial that means reporting an effect of about 1.1 SD together with the fact that items on the same test moved by amounts more than a standard deviation apart. An average effect is a claim about items as well as people.

Problems

  1. Raw effects and difficulty. Under the IL-HTE model with every \(\zeta_i = 0\), and \(\theta \sim N(0, 1)\) in control and \(N(\beta_1, 1)\) in the treated arm, write the effect on item \(i\)’s expected proportion correct as a function of \(b_i\). Show that it is largest for items near the average ability and small for very easy or very hard items. Why, then, can raw differences in proportion correct and item effects on the logit scale rank the items differently, and which would you use to say which items a program moved?
  2. How much do the flagged items carry? (PS5#4). In gilbert_meta_20 at the posttest, drop the 10 items in B or C and re-estimate the effect on the sum score and on \(\theta\). How much of the effect did they carry? Now drop instead the 10 items with the smallest estimated IL-HTE effects. Which short form would a program developer prefer, and which would you report?
  3. The interaction, with and without item slopes (PS7#1). In gilbert_meta_37, estimate the treatment-by-baseline interaction on the sum score, with a constant-effect model and with IL-HTE. From the IL-HTE fit, compute the SD of the items’ effective difficulties in each arm. Using the Go deeper, explain why the three estimates are so close here.
  4. Bereavement. After spousal loss, the CES-D items on loneliness and sadness carry most of the rise in symptoms (Domingue et al., 2019). Set out what you would have to assume to report “depression rose”, and what you would have to assume to report “the CES-D measured something different”. Given item-level data from waves before and after the loss, which model would you fit, and what result would lead you to each report?
  5. Near and far. You are designing the outcome measure for a vocabulary intervention that teaches 40 words. Design a test that can separate near transfer (taught words) from far transfer (untaught words of similar frequency), and say how you would model item-level effects so that the two can be compared. What would you report if the near items moved and the far items didn’t?
  6. Challenge: evidence for or against? Suppose the items that move most in a trial are exactly those most aligned with the intervention’s content. Is item-level heterogeneity then evidence against the measure’s validity for evaluating the intervention, evidence for it, or neither? Does your answer change if the same test is also used to report students’ general proficiency? This one is open.

Across the IRW

How common are item-level treatment effects across the trials in the warehouse? Following the IRW’s IL-HTE vignette, compute.R takes every table with a treatment indicator and at least 100 respondents, keeps the dichotomous items (at least 3), samples at most 5,000 respondents, and fits this lesson’s two models. It departs from the vignette on waves: the outcome is the first wave after randomization, read from the processing notes where they have been checked and otherwise by a stated rule, and any pretest wave is fitted too, as a placebo.

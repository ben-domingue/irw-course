What this is for

Two basketball teams play; one wins. A survey respondent is shown two risks and picks the one that did more harm. Neither setting has items, only pairs set against each other, yet the model we need is one we already have. The Rasch model compares a person with an item. Replace the item with a second person and you have the Bradley-Terry model (Bradley & Terry, 1952), a model for games, tournaments and paired-comparison judgments alike. Its online cousin, the Elo rating, produced the chess ratings in The likelihood and logistic regression. This lesson builds both from the Rasch model, then asks a question item response data rarely force on us: what if the thing measured changes while we measure it?

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • write down the Bradley-Terry model, fit it as a logistic regression, and say what fixes its origin and when its estimates exist;
  • explain why win percentage and Bradley-Terry strength agree when every agent faces the same opponents, and why they part ways when schedules differ;
  • derive the Elo update from the Bradley-Terry likelihood and say what its step size \(K\) trades off;
  • compare a static and a dynamic rating by how well each predicts games it hasn’t seen;
  • build a scale from paired-comparison judgments and say what the question asked does to it.

Core ideas

Competition data: no items, only opponents

In a competition each row pairs two agents, and the outcome says which came out ahead. The IRW stores such data in its competition standard: agent_a, agent_b, a winner (agent_a, agent_b or draw), a date, which side had the home advantage, and scores where there are scores; irw_fetch(name, source = "comp") loads them. Each outcome measures both agents at once, on one scale, and tells us only about the gap between them. The same shape fits judgments: a judge asked “which of these two is more harmful?” also produces a winner and a loser (Glickman & Jones, 2025, review models for both).

The Bradley-Terry model is a Rasch model in which the item is another agent

Recall from The Rasch model (Go deeper: sufficiency): take two items and condition on a respondent getting exactly one of them right. The probability that it was item \(i\) is \(1/(1 + e^{b_i - b_k})\), whatever the respondent’s \(\theta\). Swap the roles and compare two respondents on one item, and the comparison depends only on \(\theta_j - \theta_l\).

That comparison of two respondents is already a model for a competition. Write \(\theta_j\) for the strength of agent \(j\). The Bradley-Terry model says that when \(j\) meets \(k\), \[\Pr(j \text{ beats } k) = \frac{\exp(\theta_j - \theta_k)}{1 + \exp(\theta_j - \theta_k)}.\] Set it beside the Rasch model: the opponent’s strength \(\theta_k\) sits exactly where the item difficulty \(b_i\) sat. Zermelo (1929) proposed this model for tournament results; Bradley & Terry (1952) arrived at it for paired comparisons in experiments, and Luce (1959) reached the same form from a theory of choice behaviour. Andrich (1978) set out its relation to the Rasch model.

Anything else that tilts a game goes into the exponent. A home team has an advantage \(h\), so \(\Pr(\text{home } j \text{ beats } k) = \text{logistic}(h + \theta_j - \theta_k)\). In a judgment task, the same term captures a preference for whichever option is shown first.

Move the visitors’ strength and the curve slides without changing shape, as the Rasch curve did when we moved \(b\). The home advantage slides it too, as if every visitor were \(h\) logits weaker.

Two facts carry over from the Rasch model unchanged. The first is identification.

Recall from The Rasch model: adding the same constant to every \(\theta\) and every \(b\) leaves every probability unchanged, so the scale has no origin until we fix one.

Only differences \(\theta_j - \theta_k\) appear, so we fix one agent at 0 or make the strengths average 0. The second fact is that the model is a logistic regression: one row per game, outcome 1 if the first-listed agent won, and one column per agent, \(+1\) for the first agent, \(-1\) for the second and 0 otherwise. The intercept is \(h\). glm() fits it, as does the BradleyTerry2 package (Turner & Firth, 2012).

When does the maximum exist? Zermelo (1929) and Ford (1957) give the condition. Split the agents into any two groups; somebody in each group must have beaten somebody in the other. An agent who never loses fails it, and so does a league split into two halves that never meet.

Recall from The likelihood and logistic regression (Go deeper): if a predictor perfectly separates successes from failures, the log likelihood keeps rising as the slope grows and the MLE doesn’t exist (Albert & Anderson, 1984).

Wins are to Bradley-Terry what the sum score is to Rasch

Recall from The likelihood and logistic regression: in a logistic regression, the derivative of the log likelihood with respect to a coefficient is \(\sum_i (y_i - p_i)x_i\). For the intercept this says that at the maximum, the predicted number of successes equals the observed number.

Agent \(j\)’s column is \(+1\) in games where \(j\) is listed first and \(-1\) where it is listed second, so its derivative is zero exactly when \(j\)’s expected wins equal its actual wins. Given who played whom, the win total is sufficient for strength, as the sum score is for \(\theta\) under the Rasch model; in a round robin, ranking by strength and ranking by wins coincide. “Given who played whom” is where the two part ways.

Recall from Where CTT breaks: the sum score depends on which items were taken. A student scored more on an easy half of a test than on a hard half.

Win percentage ignores who you played; Bradley-Terry adjusts for strength of schedule, as the Rasch model adjusts for the items taken.

Elo is Bradley-Terry estimated one game at a time

Bradley-Terry fits a season at once. A chess federation has no season: games arrive daily, and a rating is wanted after each. Elo (1978) proposed a rule. Before \(j\) meets \(k\), compute from the current ratings \(\hat p = \text{logistic}(\hat\theta_j - \hat\theta_k)\). After the game, with \(y = 1\) if \(j\) won, \(\tfrac12\) for a draw and 0 for a loss, \[\hat\theta_j \leftarrow \hat\theta_j + K\,(y - \hat p), \qquad \hat\theta_k \leftarrow \hat\theta_k - K\,(y - \hat p).\] A player who wins when expected to gains a little; a player who wins as an underdog gains a lot. One rating goes up by exactly what the other goes down, so the average rating never moves, which sets the origin.

Why this rule? \(y - \hat p\) is the derivative of one game’s Bradley-Terry log likelihood with respect to \(\theta_j\) (see the Go deeper callout below), so Elo takes a step of size \(K\) uphill on the likelihood of the latest game. A small \(K\) averages over many games: stable, but slow to notice a player who improves. A large \(K\) follows change quickly, and follows luck too.

NoteElo points and logits

Chess federations report ratings in points rather than logits. In the conversion table of the FIDE Rating Regulations (§8.1.1), an expected score of 0.75, odds of 3 to 1 or \(\log 3 = 1.10\) logits, corresponds to a rating difference of 193 points, so a logit is about 175 points there. FIDE uses \(K\) = 40, 20 or 10 depending on a player’s experience and rating (§8.3.3); \(K = 20\) points is about 0.11 logits.

The same rule, with the item as the opponent, is used to track learners in adaptive practice systems (Pelánek, 2016). Glickman (1999) replaces the fixed \(K\) with a step that depends on how uncertain each rating is, so a rating we know little about moves further. That is the Glicko system.

NoteGo deeper: Elo as stochastic gradient ascent

Assumptions: games are independent given the strengths; game \(g\) between \(j\) and \(k\) follows Bradley-Terry with \(p_g = \text{logistic}(\theta_j - \theta_k)\); \(y_g \in \{0, \tfrac12, 1\}\) (a draw enters as \(\tfrac12\), which is the chess convention rather than a model for draws).

One game’s gradient. The log likelihood of game \(g\) is \(\ell_g = y_g \log p_g + (1 - y_g)\log(1 - p_g)\). As in The likelihood and logistic regression, with \(\partial p_g/\partial\theta_j = p_g(1-p_g)\), \[\frac{\partial \ell_g}{\partial \theta_j} = y_g - p_g, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell_g}{\partial \theta_k} = -(y_g - p_g),\] and every other agent’s derivative is 0. The Elo update adds \(K\) times this gradient, evaluated at the current ratings. The full log likelihood is \(\sum_g \ell_g\), so the Elo update is stochastic gradient ascent on the Bradley-Terry log likelihood: one game at a time, with learning rate \(K\).

What the fixed step does. Robbins & Monro (1951) gave a procedure of this kind, with steps that shrink as observations accumulate, whose estimates converge in probability to the value sought. Elo keeps \(K\) fixed, so its ratings never converge; they keep fluctuating around the strengths, and the fluctuations grow with \(K\). If strengths are fixed, that is a cost. If they drift, it is the point: a fixed step weights recent games more than old ones, so the ratings forget.

Two consequences. The ratings’ sum is fixed (each update adds and subtracts the same amount), so Elo sets the origin by keeping the mean at its starting value. And a home advantage, or any other term in the exponent, gets its own gradient, \(y_g - p_g\) for \(h\); problem 1 asks what updating it would do. Seen this way, Elo is one-step estimation of \(\theta\) with the opponent, or the item, treated as known. Aldous (2017) studies by simulation how accurate such ratings are.

Static or dynamic? Let prediction decide

Bradley-Terry assumes each agent has one strength for the whole period the data cover: plausible for one tournament or one survey, a strong assumption for six basketball seasons of trades and injuries. Elo assumes strength can drift, and pays for it with noise. Which assumption holds is a question the data can answer by prediction: fit on the past, predict games not yet seen, and compare the log likelihood of what happened (Glickman & Jones, 2025). In-sample fit can’t settle it, since a model with a strength per team per season fits better by construction whether or not teams changed. The NBA analysis below makes the comparison.

Paired comparisons as measurement

Thurstone (1927) used paired comparisons to scale things with no natural unit. His law of comparative judgment, in its simplest form (Case V), is Bradley-Terry with the normal curve in place of the logistic. Andrich (1978) showed that with the logistic it gives the same equations for the scale values as the Rasch model, except that the judge’s own level drops out by design, because both options go to the same judge.

What is measured here is the stimuli, not the people; the judges are instruments, and the question they were asked defines the scale. Ask “which is more harmful?” and “which could cause a bigger disaster?” of the same pairs and nothing obliges the two scales to agree. The same holds when teachers judge which of two essays is better (Pollitt, 2012): the question put to the judges plays the part of the rubric.

Code 
# Bradley-Terry as a logistic regression: one row per game, a column per team,
# +1 for the home team, -1 for the visitors. The intercept is the home advantage h.
# Only differences are identified, so we drop the first team's column (its strength
# is fixed at 0) and then re-centre the strengths to mean 0.
design <- function(d) {
  X <- matrix(0, nrow(d), length(teams), dimnames = list(NULL, teams))
  X[cbind(seq_len(nrow(d)), match(d$agent_a, teams))] <- 1
  X[cbind(seq_len(nrow(d)), match(d$agent_b, teams))] <- -1
  X
}
fit_bt <- function(d) {
  X <- design(d)
  m <- glm(d$y ~ X[, -1], family = binomial)
  theta <- c(0, coef(m)[-1])
  names(theta) <- teams
  list(h = unname(coef(m)[1]), theta = theta - mean(theta), model = m)
}
bt_all <- fit_bt(nba)
round(c(home_advantage = bt_all$h, se = summary(bt_all$model)$coefficients[1, 2]), 3)

# Elo on the logit scale: before each game, predict from the current ratings; after
# it, move both ratings by K times the surprise. Ratings start at 0 and carry over
# from season to season. The home advantage is the five-season estimate.
elo <- function(d, K, h) {
  r <- setNames(rep(0, length(teams)), teams)
  p <- numeric(nrow(d))
  for (g in seq_len(nrow(d))) {
    a <- d$agent_a[g]; b <- d$agent_b[g]
    p[g] <- plogis(h + r[a] - r[b])
    r[a] <- r[a] + K * (d$y[g] - p[g])
    r[b] <- r[b] - K * (d$y[g] - p[g])
  }
  list(p = p, ratings = r)
}

Glickman & Jones (2025) analyse NBA outcomes with dynamic models in more depth.

Where does that leave the choice? When the data span a period over which agents plausibly change, a rating that forgets, such as Elo with \(K\) chosen by prediction, may be sufficient for some purposes where a pooled fit is not. Over a short, fixed window, such as one tournament or one survey, the Bradley-Terry fit, with its standard errors and explicit terms for home advantage or order, may be the more useful summary. The first question to ask of the data is whether strength stayed put.

Code 
# The table records the chosen risk as agent_a, so we can't estimate an order
# effect. We flip a coin for which risk is written first, so that the outcome
# varies; with no intercept the fit is the same either way.
fit_judgments <- function(d, seed = 1) {
  risks <- sort(unique(c(d$agent_a, d$agent_b)))
  set.seed(seed)
  flip <- rbinom(nrow(d), 1, 0.5) == 1
  first <- ifelse(flip, d$agent_b, d$agent_a)
  second <- ifelse(flip, d$agent_a, d$agent_b)
  y <- as.integer(!flip)                              # 1 if the first risk was picked
  X <- matrix(0, nrow(d), length(risks), dimnames = list(NULL, risks))
  X[cbind(seq_len(nrow(d)), match(first, risks))] <- 1
  X[cbind(seq_len(nrow(d)), match(second, risks))] <- -1
  m <- glm(y ~ 0 + X[, -1], family = binomial)
  theta <- c(0, coef(m))
  names(theta) <- risks
  theta - mean(theta)
}

Problems

  1. Derivation. Add a home advantage to the Elo rule, \(\hat p = \text{logistic}(\hat h + \hat\theta_j - \hat\theta_k)\), and use the Go deeper callout to write an update for \(\hat h\) with its own step size. Show that the average rating still never moves. Run it on the NBA data: does \(\hat h\) settle near 0.39?
  2. Real data with a twist. Did the NBA home advantage change? Fit season-specific team strengths with one \(h\), then with a separate \(h\) per season, and compare by likelihood ratio. Would separate \(h\)s have improved the 2017-18 predictions?
  3. Judgment. A district scores teachers by the average Bradley-Terry strength of their students’ essays, from judges’ paired comparisons. What would you need to believe for these scores to be fair to teachers? Name three assumptions, and say which data like the Friedman tables could check.
  4. Design. You have 100 essays and budget for 1,000 comparisons. How would you choose the pairs? Consider when the estimates exist, how much each comparison tells you (information \(p(1-p)\)), and adaptive pairing (Pollitt, 2012). Check your design with the simulation.
  5. Real data. Friedman’s other criteria are IRW tables with the same layout, such as friedman2019_risk_worry. Fit one and compare it with the harm scale. Which risks move most, and why might they? Check the wording in the codebook first.
  6. Challenge (open). The Friedman raters disagree. Split them by a feature you can justify (the replication data hold respondent covariates), fit a mixture of Bradley-Terry models, or give raters random effects. Is there one scale of harm, or several? What would count as evidence either way?

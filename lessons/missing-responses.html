What this is for

A fourth grader answers the first eight questions on a reading passage, leaves the ninth blank, answers the tenth and eleventh, and writes nothing after that. What should the blank on item 9 count for, and the four at the end? Most analyses in this course have left gaps missing, a choice that rests on why a response is missing. This lesson separates responses that are omitted (seen and skipped) from those not reached (never got to), recovers the two from the order of the items in an IRW table that doesn’t record them, and asks, in PIRLS 2023 reading data, which kind carries information about ability. It picks up from Estimating abilities, because the EAP’s pull toward the mean is part of what a missing response does to a score.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • classify a kind of missing response as MCAR, MAR or MNAR, and say what the long table can’t tell you;
  • explain why marginal maximum likelihood can ignore gaps left by a booklet design or a stopping rule, and when it can’t;
  • show what scoring an omit as wrong, or leaving it missing, does to one respondent’s estimate;
  • recover omitted and not-reached responses from item order, and check the rule against a source that records them;
  • fit the same data under several treatments of the gaps, including a response-propensity model, and report how far the estimates move.

Core ideas

Why a response is missing is part of the data

Recall from Item response data and the IRW: a missing response is an absent row in a long table, and it becomes an empty cell when the table is reshaped. Skipped at random, missing by booklet design and stopped early leave different patterns of gaps, and the table alone doesn’t say which produced a given gap.

Write \(m_{ij} = 1\) when respondent \(j\)’s response to item \(i\) is missing, so that every cell carries a pair \((x_{ij}, m_{ij})\), with \(x_{ij}\) unseen when \(m_{ij} = 1\). Rubin (1976) sorted the ways the \(m_{ij}\) can arise into three kinds (Little & Rubin, 2019; Schafer & Graham, 2002):

  • Missing completely at random (MCAR): missingness has nothing to do with the respondent or the item, as when a page is lost in the post.
  • Missing at random (MAR): it depends only on what we observe. A booklet design, an adaptive test (the next item depends on the answers so far) and a stopping rule on the first half of a test are MAR.
  • Missing not at random (MNAR): it depends on something unobserved, such as the response itself (“skip it if I’d get it wrong”) or \(\theta\) (weaker readers skip more).

The labels describe the process that produced the gaps, not the gaps (Seaman, Galati, Jackson & Carlin, 2013, state them with care). The design and the source’s missing codes say which kind we have; the long table rarely does.

Ignorable: when the likelihood can leave the gaps alone

Why does the mechanism matter for a fitted model? Rubin (1976) showed that if the gaps are MAR, and the parameters of the missingness process are distinct from the model’s, the likelihood of the observed responses alone gives the right answer: the process is ignorable (Mislevy & Wu, 1996, and Mislevy, 2017, work this out for item response models). The marginal likelihood that mirt maximizes (Bock & Aitkin, 1981; Chalmers, 2012) is that likelihood: an empty cell contributes nothing. So booklet designs and adaptive tests are ignorable, although an adaptive test’s gaps depend on ability through the answers already given. The Go deeper callout writes out the argument.

Deleting incomplete respondents (listwise deletion) is also unbiased under MCAR, at the cost of the data it discards; in a booklet design it discards everyone (in the TIMSS 2007 data checked below, no student saw every item). Finch (2008) compares treatments of missing responses for item parameters.

If you’ve done Confirmatory factor analysis: lavaan’s missing = "ml" (full-information ML) is the same idea for factor models (Enders & Bandalos, 2001). If you’ve done Estimating item parameters: EM treats \(\theta\) itself as missing data.

NoteGo deeper: why MAR gaps drop out of the likelihood, and when they don’t

Assumptions: responses \(x\) follow an item response model with item parameters \(\beta\) and a population distribution \(g(\theta)\); the indicators \(m\) follow a model with parameters \(\phi\); write \(x = (x_{\text{obs}}, x_{\text{mis}})\). (Here \(\beta\) collects every item parameter, not regression coefficients.)

The factorization. The observed data are \((x_{\text{obs}}, m)\), and their probability averages over what we didn’t see: \[p(x_{\text{obs}}, m \mid \beta, \phi) = \int\!\!\int p(x_{\text{obs}}, x_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta, \beta)\, p(m \mid x_{\text{obs}}, x_{\text{mis}}, \theta, \phi)\, g(\theta)\, dx_{\text{mis}}\, d\theta.\] Under MAR, \(p(m \mid x_{\text{obs}}, x_{\text{mis}}, \theta, \phi) = p(m \mid x_{\text{obs}}, \phi)\): the gaps depend only on what we observe. That factor then leaves both integrals, and \[p(x_{\text{obs}}, m \mid \beta, \phi) = p(m \mid x_{\text{obs}}, \phi) \int p(x_{\text{obs}} \mid \theta, \beta)\, g(\theta)\, d\theta.\] If \(\phi\) and \(\beta\) are distinct (no restriction ties them), the first factor is a constant as far as \(\beta\) is concerned, and maximizing the second, the marginal likelihood of the observed responses, is all we need.

Where it breaks. If the gaps depend on \(\theta\), as when lower-ability respondents skip more, then \(p(m \mid \cdot)\) contains \(\theta\) and can’t leave the integral over it. For the marginal likelihood this is MNAR, although no single response is hidden from the mechanism. It becomes ignorable again if we add a second latent variable \(\xi\) for the propensity to respond, correlated with \(\theta\): given \((\theta, \xi)\), the gaps depend on nothing unobserved. That is the premise of the propensity models in this lesson (Holman & Glas, 2005). Gaps that depend on the unseen response itself (“skip it if wrong”) remain MNAR even then.

Why it is load-bearing. Concurrent calibration across booklets (Linking and equating), adaptive testing (Item banks and adaptive testing) and a skip node in a tree (IRTrees) all rest on this factorization.

Omitted: wrong, missing, or something in between?

A student who reads an item and leaves it blank has omitted it. The two simple treatments are each an assumption: scoring it wrong assumes the student would have got it wrong, and leaving it missing assumes the omit says nothing about \(\theta\). Lord (1974) argued that the first is not justified when estimating abilities or item parameters, and gave a method that uses what omits reveal. Treatments are compared in simulation by De Ayala, Plake and Impara (2001) and reviewed for large-scale assessments by Robitzsch (2021).

Recall from Estimating abilities: the EAP is pulled toward the prior mean, most where the likelihood is flattest, which includes short tests; and a respondent who gets every item right has no maximum-likelihood estimate.

Leaving an omit missing shortens the respondent’s test, so both apply. A respondent who answers only the items they know can have a perfect pattern on those items, and no MLE; their EAP is pulled toward the population mean, which moves a below-average respondent up.

Modelling the propensity to respond

If omitting goes with ability, can a model use it? The latent-ignorability models add a second latent variable, the response propensity \(\xi_j\), which drives the indicators \(m_{ij}\) and correlates with \(\theta_j\) (Holman & Glas, 2005; Rose, von Davier & Xu, 2010). Given both, the gaps are ignorable, and the correlation carries what omitting says about \(\theta\). The model of Mislevy and Wu (1996) also lets an omit depend on the response the student would have given; scoring omits wrong and latent ignorability are special cases of it (Rose, von Davier & Nagengast, 2017). Each rests on an assumption about unseen responses that the data can’t check directly. On the German National Educational Panel Study’s competence tests, the model-based treatments and leaving the gaps missing gave similar results, and scoring omits wrong different ones (Pohl, Gräfe & Rose, 2014; Köhler, Pohl & Carstensen, 2015).

If you’ve done IRTrees: a first node “answered?” is a propensity model (Debeer, Janssen & De Boeck, 2017). There, simulated heavy skippers came out 0.24 too low with skips wrong, 0.41 too high with skips missing, and 0.02 off with the skip node.

Not reached: time, not knowledge

A not-reached item sits at the end of a timed part the respondent never finished, and it says something about speed, which may or may not go with ability. The IEA, which runs TIMSS and PIRLS, defines it by position: within a part of the booklet, an unanswered item is not reached if the item before it is also unanswered and no later item in the part is answered (Foy, Galia & Li, 2008, §11.3.3). So the first blank of a trailing run counts as omitted, and a run of one is an omit. TIMSS 2007 treated not-reached items as not administered when it estimated item parameters, and as wrong when it scored students (the same section).

Why treat them differently for items? Scored wrong, an item late in a timed test looks harder than it is. In TIMSS 2003 the same item looked harder when it came late in a booklet than when it came early (the same report, §11.3.1), and Oshima (1994) showed the effect in simulation. Speeded respondents can also be modelled as a latent class (Bolt, Cohen & Wollack, 2002) or with a propensity for speed (Glas & Pimentel, 2008); van der Linden (2018) treats speededness in full.

The IEA rule needs only the order in which items were presented and where each timed part ends. The IRW keeps the gaps, so where the order is known the rule works on a table that records no codes at all.

A quarter of the cells are empty, and 36% of learners have no gap. Is a gap an omit or a sign of running out of time? We treat the passage as one timed part and apply the IEA rule in item order.

Code 
# The IEA's rule, applied within one timed part: an unanswered item is not reached if
# the item before it is also unanswered and nothing after it is answered. So the
# first item of a trailing run of gaps counts as omitted, and a run of one is an omit.
classify <- function(miss) {           # miss: TRUE/FALSE per item, in the order given
  last <- max(c(0, which(!miss)))      # position of the last answered item
  nr <- seq_along(miss) > last + 1     # after the last answer, and after the first gap
  ifelse(!miss, "answered", ifelse(nr, "not reached", "omitted"))
}
# Missing-data choice: the whole passage is treated as one timed part, in item order.
code <- t(apply(is.na(Y), 1, classify))
dimnames(code) <- dimnames(Y)

What do the treatments do? We fit the partial credit model (Masters, 1982) with every gap wrong, every gap missing, and a split by the rule (rule in the code): omits 0, and not-reached items missing, as TIMSS calibrates them.

What each treatment assumes

Each treatment is an assumption. Leaving omits missing assumes they say nothing about \(\theta\); scoring them 0, that the respondent would have got them wrong; a propensity model, that the correlation between answering and ability carries what omitting reveals. Treating not-reached items as not administered assumes the student would have answered them like those who reached them; scoring them wrong counts running out of time as part of the performance.

Some of these assumptions the data can speak to. Whether omitting goes with ability is observable: in PIRLS 2023 South Africa it does, weakly (−0.16 with the plausible value, and a propensity correlation of 0.30), so the simple treatments of omits part company for the learners who omit most (−0.31 left missing, −0.56 scored 0, −0.46 with the propensity model). Where omitting is rare and unrelated to ability, they would barely differ. What an omitted response would have been is never observed, so no fit settles the last step; how far the estimates move across treatments shows how much a conclusion leans on it. For not-reached items, the TIMSS rule (missing when calibrating, wrong when scoring) separates what an item is from what a respondent did, and each half of it rests on one of the assumptions above.

Problems

  1. The factorization. For a Rasch test, show that under MAR with distinct \(\phi\), \(m\) drops out of the likelihood for the item parameters. Then show where the argument breaks when omitting depends on \(\theta\) and the likelihood is marginal over \(\theta\).
  2. Undo the scoring. In borges_brazil_residency_2024_cbt, set resp to missing where resp_raw is empty and fit a Rasch model both ways. Whose estimates move, and how far? Are the X’s of the 18 applicants with ten or more at the end of the exam, or scattered?
  3. Which comparison to report. In PIRLS 2011, the Netherlands–France difference is 0.46, 0.28 or 0.43 SD, depending on the scoring. Which would you report in a comparison of the two countries, and what else would you want to see first?
  4. Formats. In PIRLS 2023, compare the omission rates of the four multiple-choice and eleven constructed-response items. The source’s code 9 means “omitted or invalid”. What might explain the difference, and what does each scoring do to a comparison of formats?
  5. Design a record. For a low-stakes online test with a 20-minute limit, what would you record (timestamps, the order each respondent saw, a “skip” button) so that omitted and not-reached items can be told apart without an order rule?
  6. Challenge (open): what an omit would have been. Whether an omitted item would have been answered correctly is never observed. What assumption lets the Mislevy–Wu model estimate it, and could any design check that assumption? As far as I know, this is not settled.

For instructors

  • Source: no EDUC 252 class covers missing responses; the lesson is new (#153). The data audit in outlines/missing-responses.md records how the IRW represents missing responses and which tables keep the codes.
  • Timing: the core ideas and widgets take about 60 minutes; the PIRLS 2023 analysis works as a 30-minute lab. Every fit takes seconds in local R, the propensity model about ten.
  • Data: the PIRLS 2023 check downloads the source file (4.5 MB, CC BY 4.0) from the University of Pretoria’s repository. Only the check and the plausible value depend on it; everything else uses the IRW table alone. The TIMSS 2007 check needs sirt.
  • Held back: rapid guessing as a missing-data screen (Mixture models); plausible values with background conditioning, which is how TIMSS and PIRLS score students; missingness by design in adaptive testing (Item banks and adaptive testing). ENEM tables carry each candidate’s item order, but blanks are rare there (a blank earns what a wrong answer earns), so they stay in the outline.
  • Solutions: held back in solutions/missing-responses.qmd (not published).

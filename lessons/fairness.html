What this is for

On an online vocabulary checklist, respondents aged 40 and over check more words than respondents under 25, and native speakers of English check more than non-native speakers. Is the checklist unfair to the young, or to non-native speakers? The gaps alone can’t say. Validity as an argument called anything in a score that has nothing to do with the construct construct-irrelevant variance; when it lines up with group membership, it is a question of fairness. This lesson separates a difference in the construct (impact) from a difference in how the measure works (bias), asks what it means for a measure to be the same measure in two groups (measurement invariance), and looks at bias in what scores predict. By the end, the checklist gives two different answers for its two groupings.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • describe the four views of fairness in the Standards, and say why a group difference in scores is not, by itself, evidence of bias;
  • distinguish impact from bias, and name where bias can enter (the instrument, its administration, culture);
  • say what configural, metric and scalar invariance require, which comparison each supports, and what no invariance test can detect;
  • define predictive bias, and explain why an unreliable test can look biased in prediction when it isn’t;
  • check invariance for a whole scale with a multigroup model, and judge whether a failure changes the comparison you care about.

Core ideas

What does fairness mean?

Recall from Validity as an argument: validity is the degree to which evidence supports an interpretation of scores for a use. The Standards call anything in scores that is irrelevant to the construct construct-irrelevant variance, with the example of a mathematics test that leans on reading English. The first thing we want from a measure, from What is measurement?, is insensitivity to attributes other than its target.

The Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing treat fairness as a question of validity (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, ch. 3, pp. 49–51). A fair test measures the same construct for everyone it is meant for, so a score means the same thing whoever earned it. The chapter sets out four views (pp. 51–54):

  1. Fair treatment during testing: standard conditions, with flexibility where a standard procedure would keep someone from showing their standing.
  2. Lack of measurement bias: scores mean the same in different groups, item by item, for the whole test, and in what they predict.
  3. Access to the construct: nothing irrelevant (small print, idioms, unfamiliar contexts) stands between a respondent and the tasks.
  4. Validity of individual interpretations: group labels are a convenience; a score is interpreted for the person.

What doesn’t count? The chapter explicitly sets aside one view of fairness, that it means equal outcomes across groups. Differences should prompt a search for sources of bias, but “group differences in outcomes do not in themselves indicate that a testing application is biased or unfair” (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, p. 54). This lesson is about the second view, measurement bias, the one data can speak to most directly (Camilli, 2013, takes up the others).

Impact is not bias

Measure the heights of 3-year-olds and 10-year-olds. The older children are taller, and nobody blames the tape measure. That is impact: the groups differ on the attribute itself. Bias is a difference in scores that comes from something other than the attribute: say, the 10-year-olds were measured with their shoes on. Put in terms of error, a measure is fair to two groups when its measurement error is unrelated to group membership. Impact lives in the attribute; bias lives in the error.

Observed scores alone can’t split a gap into impact and bias; that takes a comparison at the same level of the attribute, or a criterion outside the test.

Where does bias come from? From the instrument: an item that leans on knowledge or language one group is less likely to have, as with the mathematics test that leans on reading. From administration: when respondents to the Health and Retirement Study were randomly assigned to answer its cognitive items on the web or by phone, those on the web scored higher, on every item (Domingue et al., 2023). Random assignment made the two groups comparable in cognition, so the difference came from the mode. And from culture: Kroupin et al. (2025) argue that standard tasks of executive function draw on skills that develop mainly with formal schooling, so they may not measure the same thing in children who haven’t been to school.

A rule of mine: psychological measures are at their best comparing people who start in similar situations. The bigger the impact, the harder bias is to find, because our checks compare respondents at the same level of the attribute, and when groups barely overlap there are few of them.

The same measure in different groups

What does it take for a measure to be the same in two groups? Measurement invariance: the distribution of responses given the attribute doesn’t depend on the group (Meredith, 1993; Millsap, 2011). When it holds, a difference in scores between groups is impact. When it fails for the test as a whole, the Standards speak of differential test functioning (DTF): members of different groups with the same standing don’t have the same expected score (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, p. 51).

In practice invariance is checked by fitting a model to both groups at once and adding constraints in steps (Vandenberg & Lance, 2000; Putnick & Bornstein, 2016):

  • configural: the same items measure the same attribute in both groups, with every parameter free;
  • metric: each item’s slope (or loading) is equal across groups, so a unit on the scale means the same thing in each; this is enough to compare correlations;
  • scalar: each item’s intercept (or difficulty) is equal too, so respondents at the same level have the same expected response; this is what comparing means needs.

When one or two items fail, partial invariance frees their parameters and keeps the rest equal.

If you’ve done Factor analysis II: this is the multigroup ladder you fitted with lavaan to the DASS-21, where equal loadings and equal intercepts cost almost nothing across women and men, so their factor means could be compared. Here the same ladder is fitted with an item response model.

These checks have a limit. The model places the two groups on one scale using the items themselves, since the attribute is never observed. If every item were harder for the focal group by the same amount, its respondents would answer exactly as if they were that much lower on the attribute.

Bias in prediction

If invariance checks can’t see a bias shared by every item, what can? Evidence from outside the test. Predictive bias is present when scores consistently predict too high or too low a criterion for members of a group (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, glossary). Cleary (1968) framed it with regression: fit one line predicting the criterion (college grades, say) from the test, and ask whether a group’s criterion sits systematically above or below it. The Standards ask for this analysis wherever scores are used to predict and samples allow, by comparing slopes and intercepts or deviations from a common line, and note that comparing correlations is not enough when groups differ in means and variances (Standard 3.7, pp. 65–66).

If you’ve done Gathering validity evidence: predictive bias is criterion evidence compared across groups rather than across a selection.

Recall from Classical test theory and reliability: reliability is the share of observed-score variance that is true-score variance. The rest is error.

Start with reliability 1 and no disadvantage: the three lines nearly coincide and the focal group’s average residual is close to 0. Lower reliability to 0.7. The focal group’s line drops below the reference group’s and the common line over-predicts the focal group by 0.14, though nothing about the criterion has changed. Each group’s scores regress toward its own mean, so a given test score means a lower true standing in the lower-scoring group. Linn and Werts (1971) discuss this effect of unreliability on studies of predictive bias (the Go deeper below derives it). Now add a real disadvantage of 0.3 at reliability 1: the common line over-predicts again, by 0.12, for a reason that has nothing to do with error.

NoteGo deeper: why a fallible test over-predicts the lower group

Assumptions. In group \(g\), \(\theta \sim N(\mu_g, 1)\). The test score is \(X = \theta + e\) with \(e \sim N(0, \sigma_e^2)\), so its reliability within a group is \(\rho = 1/(1 + \sigma_e^2)\). The criterion is \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1\theta + u\), the same in every group, with \(u\) independent of \(\theta\) and \(e\). There is no bias of any kind.

The within-group regression. Within group \(g\), \((\theta, X)\) is bivariate normal, so \(E(\theta \mid X, g) = \mu_g + \rho\,(X - \mu_g)\). Then \[E(Y \mid X, g) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\rho X + \beta_1(1 - \rho)\,\mu_g.\] The slopes are equal (\(\beta_1\rho\)) and the intercepts differ by \(\beta_1(1-\rho)(\mu_1 - \mu_2)\).

What it means. At reliability 1 the intercepts agree; at any lower reliability, the group with the lower mean has the lower line. A common line fitted to both groups lies between the two, so it over-predicts the lower group and under-predicts the higher, with no bias anywhere. With \(\beta_1 = 1\), \(\rho = 0.7\) and a gap of 1, the intercepts differ by 0.3, and the common line’s average error for each group is somewhat smaller than that (0.14 in the widget, with equal group sizes).

Where else it appears. Any comparison of groups at the same observed score inherits the same shrinkage toward group means. Differential item functioning meets it when it matches respondents on their total score.

The outside criterion is what makes a shared shift visible: a test that understates one group on every item passes an invariance check but under-predicts that group’s criterion (see the simulation). The two kinds of evidence can also disagree: a test can show no measurement bias yet differ between groups in its regression lines, and the reverse (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, p. 52; Millsap, 2007).

Item by item

The third way goes item by item: differential item functioning (DIF), when respondents at the same level of the attribute differ, by group, in their chance of a response to one item (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, p. 51). It is the item-level case of invariance and inherits its limit: an item works differently only relative to the others. A flagged item is not yet a biased one; that takes an explanation. Differential item functioning takes this up.

Simulate

The code makes two groups that differ by impact = 0.8 SD, answering 15 items that work the same way for both. It prints the raw gaps, compares configural and scalar models, and regresses a criterion that depends on \(\theta\) alone on the sum score. The first run takes 20–30 seconds while R and mirt load.

With the seed as written, every item’s raw gap is negative (from −0.08 to −0.21) and the sum scores differ by 0.65 SD, with no biased item. The invariance test finds nothing (\(\chi^2\) = 11.9 on 28 df, \(p\) = 1.00) and the scalar model puts the focal group’s mean at −0.79, close to the true −0.8. Yet the common regression line over-predicts the focal group’s criterion: the intercept difference is −0.13 (\(t\) = −4.0), the Go deeper’s artefact. Set ni <- 5 and it grows to −0.29; with ni <- 40, −0.10.

Now set shift_all <- 0.5, a bias on every item. The invariance test still finds nothing (\(p\) = 0.85), the focal group’s mean moves to −1.31, and its criterion is now under-predicted (+0.26). With shift_one <- 1 instead, the invariance test rejects (\(p\) < .001).

How big is a departure from invariance when nothing differs? The function below fits configural, scalar and partial models (each word gets a slope and a difficulty, as in From the 1PL to the 4PL); we first split the respondents at random, ten times.

Code 
# Configural: a 2PL for each group, every slope and intercept free. Scalar: the
# same slopes and intercepts in both groups, with the second group's mean and
# variance free (the first group's are fixed at 0 and 1 to set the scale).
# Partial: scalar, except that the words in `free` get their own slope and
# intercept in each group. mirt writes the 2PL as a*theta + d.
invariance <- function(Y, g, ref, free = character()) {
  keep <- !is.na(g)
  G <- factor(g[keep], levels = c(ref, setdiff(unique(g[keep]), ref)))
  fit <- function(inv) multipleGroup(Y[keep, ], 1, group = G, itemtype = "2PL",
                                     invariance = inv, verbose = FALSE)
  eq <- c("free_means", "free_var")
  out <- list(configural = fit(character()),
              scalar = fit(c("slopes", "intercepts", eq)))
  if (length(free)) out$partial <- fit(c(setdiff(colnames(Y), free), eq))
  out
}
latent_gap <- function(m) round(coef(m, simplify = TRUE)[[2]]$means[1], 2)
# The likelihood-ratio test and information criteria, one model against the configural.
compare <- function(fits, what = "scalar") {
  out <- anova(fits[[what]], fits$configural)
  rownames(out) <- c(what, "configural")
  round(out, 1)
}

Across ten random splits, the likelihood-ratio \(\chi^2\) for the scalar constraints runs from 18.6 to 54.2 on 24 df, and 4 of the 10 fall below .05, where 1 in 20 would be expected. BIC, which charges each parameter more heavily, favours the scalar model in all ten, by 134 to 169. With these 13 words, mostly near the ceiling, the \(\chi^2\) test rejects more often than its nominal rate; the random splits are our baseline.

What should a practitioner do with a failed invariance test? One position treats scalar invariance as a gate: if the test rejects, don’t compare means. It assumes the test’s null is the right standard, and in these data the test rejected in 4 of 10 random splits. Another treats non-invariance as a matter of degree, sized by what it does to the comparison. It assumes the items left equal are themselves invariant, since they set the scale. Where I come down: when an invariance test rejects, I don’t stop at the \(p\)-value. I free the items that fail and ask whether the comparison I care about moves. By age it barely moves (1.25 to 1.19); by native language it moves by a quarter, which is too much to ignore.

Is the native-language gap, then, bias? That depends on the use. If the scores are read as knowledge of English words, the ten anchor words suggest most of the gap is impact, and the three words that depart are measuring something extra for some respondents. If the same scores were used as a measure of verbal ability in an international sample, the whole gap would be construct-irrelevant: English is part of every item, a shift shared by all of them, and no invariance test would see it.

Problems

  1. What invariance can’t see. Show that under the 2PL, adding \(\delta\) to every item’s difficulty for one group gives the same response probabilities as lowering that group’s \(\theta\) by \(\delta\). What does this imply about a translation that makes every item harder, and what kind of evidence could detect it?
  2. The non-words. The three non-words should never be checked. Compare how often they are checked by age and by native language. Drop everyone who checks any and refit the native-language comparison: does the gap change, and do the same three words depart?
  3. One gap, two uses. The checklist is used (a) to study English vocabulary across adulthood in English-speaking countries, and (b) as a covariate for verbal ability in an international online study. For each use, is the native-language gap impact, bias, or both? Which of the Standards’ four views does each answer draw on?
  4. Web or phone? The Health and Retirement Study randomly assigned respondents to answer by web or by phone (Domingue et al., 2023). Why does random assignment reveal a mode effect shared by every item, which an invariance test can’t? What would you do without randomization?
  5. How big is the artefact? In the simulation, \(\beta_1 = 1\), the groups differ by 0.8 SD, and the 15-item sum score’s reliability is about 0.8 (compute alpha within each group to check). How far apart does the Go deeper put the two intercepts? The simulation gives −0.13: why might a sum score of 2PL items not match exactly?
  6. Challenge: invariant and unfair? Meredith (1993) argues that strict factorial invariance is required for fairness. Suppose a test is invariant across two groups and the gap between them reflects unequal opportunity to learn the material (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, p. 54). Is using its scores to select students fair? Is invariance necessary for fairness, sufficient, or neither? This is open.

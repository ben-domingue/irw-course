What this is for

A respondent answers all ten items of a vocabulary test correctly. What is their ability? The likelihood, which has given us every estimate so far, says it is larger than any number you care to name. Abilities come to us only through an estimator, and the estimators disagree most for the respondents a test measures worst, at the top and the bottom. With the item parameters known, this lesson starts from maximum likelihood, shows where it has no answer, brings in a prior to get the expected a posteriori (EAP) estimate, and fits four estimators to the Wordsum vocabulary test, where 12% of respondents have no maximum-likelihood estimate at all.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • write down the likelihood for one respondent’s \(\theta\) with the item parameters known, find its maximum, and give its standard error;
  • explain why a perfect pattern has no maximum-likelihood estimate, and how the alternatives stay finite;
  • compute an EAP estimate on a grid of nodes, and explain why it is pulled toward the mean and by how much;
  • say what the MLE, WLE, MAP and EAP each assume, and choose among them for reporting scores to individuals and for comparing groups;
  • show that under the Rasch model every one of these estimates is a function of the sum score, and that under the 2PL it isn’t.

Core ideas

One respondent, one unknown

Recall from The Rasch model: mirt estimates the item parameters first, then abilities treating them as known.

This lesson is the second step. With the item parameters known, a respondent’s answers are independent Bernoulli trials whose probabilities depend on one unknown, \(\theta\): a logistic regression with the respondent’s items as the observations (Lord, 1980). For the 2PL, \[\ell(\theta) = \sum_{i} \Big[x_i \log P_i(\theta) + (1 - x_i)\log\big(1 - P_i(\theta)\big)\Big], \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} = \sum_i a_i\big(x_i - P_i(\theta)\big),\] and the Rasch model sets every \(a_i = 1\).

Recall from The likelihood and logistic regression: at the maximum the derivative of the log likelihood (the score equation) is zero, and the curvature gives the standard error. With an intercept, the score equation says the predicted number of successes equals the observed number.

Here \(\theta\) is that intercept. Under the Rasch model the maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE) solves \(\sum_i P_i(\hat\theta) = r\): the expected number correct equals the observed number. Software solves it by Newton–Raphson, stepping from a guess by the ratio of the derivative to the curvature (Baker & Kim, 2004).

Recall from Information, precision, and short forms: the negative curvature of the log likelihood is the test information, \(I(\theta) = \sum_i a_i^2 P_i(1 - P_i)\), and the conditional standard error of measurement is \(SE(\theta) = 1/\sqrt{I(\theta)}\). For one respondent’s estimate, evaluate it at their \(\hat\theta\).

So the standard error of the MLE is \(1/\sqrt{I(\hat\theta)}\), a large-sample approximation that holds up better in the middle of a test than at its ends.

Perfect patterns have no maximum

Why does the all-correct pattern never peak? Each item’s probability of a correct answer rises with \(\theta\), so their product does too. The score equation asks the expected number correct to equal the number of items, which it approaches but never reaches. The MLE is \(+\infty\), and for an all-wrong pattern \(-\infty\).

Recall from The likelihood and logistic regression, its Go deeper callout: if a predictor perfectly separates successes from failures, the logistic regression MLE doesn’t exist (Albert & Anderson, 1984). It named a respondent who answers every item correctly as the item-response version.

It is the coin with ten heads in ten flips: \(\hat p = 1\), whose log odds is infinite. mirt reports \(\pm\)Inf; optimize() on \([-10, 10]\) returns the bound, a number that looks like an estimate (the simulation below shows it). One fix that needs no prior is the weighted likelihood estimate (WLE), which is finite for every pattern under the Rasch model, the 2PL and even the 3PL and 4PL (Magis & Verhelst, 2017); it has its own subsection below.

NoteGo deeper: why perfect patterns have no MLE, and what keeps the alternatives finite

Assumptions: one respondent answers \(I\) dichotomous items under the 2PL (the Rasch model is \(a_i = 1\)), with the item parameters known and responses locally independent given \(\theta\). Write \(P_i = \text{logit}^{-1}\big(a_i(\theta - b_i)\big)\).

No maximum. If every \(x_i = 1\), the log likelihood is \(\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \log P_i(\theta)\), and \[\frac{d\ell}{d\theta} = \sum_i a_i\,\big(1 - P_i(\theta)\big) > 0 \quad\text{for every } \theta.\] The log likelihood is strictly increasing, bounded above by 0, and approaches 0 as \(\theta \to \infty\). It has a least upper bound but no maximum. The all-wrong pattern is the mirror image. This is separation in the sense of Albert and Anderson (1984): with \(\theta\) as the only parameter, a pattern with no variation is perfectly separated.

The WLE stays finite. Warm (1989) adds a term to the score equation that removes the first-order bias of the MLE. For the Rasch model it is \[\sum_i (x_i - P_i) + \frac{J(\theta)}{2I(\theta)} = 0, \qquad J(\theta) = \sum_i P_i(1 - P_i)(1 - 2P_i),\quad I(\theta) = \sum_i P_i(1 - P_i).\] For a perfect score the first term is \(\sum_i (1 - P_i)\), which falls to 0 as \(\theta\) grows, while the second tends to \(-1/2\): as every \(P_i \to 1\), \(1 - 2P_i \to -1\) and \(J \to -I\). As \(\theta\) falls, the first term tends to \(I\), the number of items, and the second to \(+1/2\). The left-hand side is positive at the bottom of the scale and negative at the top, so it has a root: the WLE is finite. Magis and Verhelst (2017) prove this for every response pattern under the one- to four-parameter logistic models.

The EAP and MAP stay finite. With a normal prior, the log posterior is \(\ell(\theta) - (\theta - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)\) plus a constant. The first term is bounded above by 0 and the second falls without limit, so the log posterior has a maximum (the MAP). The posterior is a bounded function times a normal density, so it has a finite mean (the EAP).

Why it matters later. Joint maximum likelihood, which estimates items and respondents together, has to set perfect patterns aside before it starts (Estimating item parameters). An adaptive test estimates \(\theta\) after one or two items, when all-correct patterns are the rule (Item banks and adaptive testing). And a reported score has to say something about everyone at the ceiling (What does a score mean?).

Bring in a prior: the EAP estimate

The likelihood lesson set aside the Bayesian reading, in which \(\theta\) gets a prior distribution, \(g(\theta)\), and the data turn it into a posterior, \[p(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{L(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})\,g(\theta)}{\int L(t \mid \mathbf{x})\,g(t)\,dt}.\] For ability, the natural prior is the distribution of \(\theta\) in the respondent’s population, which mirt estimates with the items (a normal with mean 0 and an SD fixed at 1 or estimated). The expected a posteriori (EAP) estimate is the posterior mean, and its standard error is the posterior SD (Bock & Mislevy, 1982). The integral needs no closed form: evaluate likelihood × prior at nodes \(t_1, \dots, t_Q\) and average, \[\hat\theta_{\text{EAP}} = \frac{\sum_q t_q\,L(t_q)\,g(t_q)}{\sum_q L(t_q)\,g(t_q)}.\] Nothing iterates and nothing fails: a normal prior times a bounded likelihood always has a mean, even when the likelihood never peaks. The posterior’s peak is the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate, also called Bayes modal (Mislevy, 1986).

MAP, EAP and plausible values

The MAP sits close to the EAP; it is found by Newton–Raphson with the prior adding a term to the score equation, and suits software that finds modes more easily than means. If the prior describes the population, the EAP has the smallest mean squared error averaged over that population, since squared error is what a posterior mean minimizes; shrinkage is the other side of that coin.

No individual estimate carries the spread of \(\theta\) itself (EAPs vary too little, MLEs too much), so variances, correlations and regressions built on them are off. Large-scale assessments such as NAEP use plausible values (Mislevy, 1991; Mislevy, Beaton, Kaplan & Sheehan, 1992): several random draws from each respondent’s posterior, under a prior that is a regression of \(\theta\) on background variables. Each set has the spread and group differences of \(\theta\); the analysis is run on each and combined as in multiple imputation. They are a tool for population quantities, not scores to report for individuals (Wu, 2005). What does a score mean? takes them up in full.

The weighted likelihood estimate

What if we want an estimate that borrows nothing from the population but exists for every pattern? Warm (1989) weights the likelihood by a function of \(\theta\) chosen to cancel the leading term of the MLE’s bias, the bias that Lord (1983) derives. This adds \(J(\theta)/2I(\theta)\) to the score equation (the Go deeper callout writes it out). The WLE has the MLE’s large-sample variance, less bias, and a finite value for every pattern (Magis & Verhelst, 2017). It is Firth’s (1993) bias reduction, which penalizes the likelihood by the Jeffreys prior and also gives finite logistic regressions under separation (Heinze & Schemper, 2002); for the Rasch model and 2PL the WLE is the peak of the likelihood times that prior, while under the 3PL the two part (Magis & Raîche, 2012).

How does it compare? Warm’s Monte Carlo studies favoured it over the MLE and Bayes modal estimates on several criteria across a wide range of abilities, and Kim and Nicewander (1993) found the WLE, MAP and EAP all close to unbiased where the test’s items are. That is the evidence: less bias than the MLE at the same variance, and finiteness, not optimality in general. The cost is the EAP’s lower error. The WLE doesn’t shrink, so in the simulation below its RMSE is 0.77 against the EAP’s 0.61, and its SD 1.28 against a true 1. It suits estimates that must not depend on a population prior, as when respondents come from different populations.

Sufficiency, revisited

Recall from The Rasch model: under the Rasch model the sum score \(r\) is sufficient for \(\theta\). The likelihood factorizes into \(e^{r\theta}\), times a term free of \(\theta\), over a term free of the pattern.

Every estimator here uses the responses only through the likelihood, so under the Rasch model every one depends on them only through \(r\): the score equations contain the data only as \(r\), and in the posterior the pattern’s own factor cancels. Ten Wordsum items give eleven possible EAPs, which is how the widgets above could list every estimate.

Recall from From the 1PL to the 4PL: under the 2PL the likelihood depends on the pattern only through the weighted score \(\sum_i a_i x_i\), which is therefore sufficient; the score equation is \(\sum_i a_i (x_i - P_i) = 0\).

So respondents with the same sum score but different items right get different estimates, ordered by \(\sum_i a_i x_i\).

Recall from From the 1PL to the 4PL, its Go deeper callout: under the 3PL the weight of each correct answer depends on \(\theta\), so no simple statistic is sufficient. There an estimate depends on the whole pattern, not on a weighted sum.

Simulate

Which estimator lands closest to the truth? The code below simulates Rasch responses to ten items (\(b\) from −2 to 2) from 5,000 respondents in two groups whose means differ by 1 (overall mean 0, SD 1), estimates every \(\theta\) four ways with the difficulties known and a normal(0, 1) prior, and reports bias and root mean squared error (RMSE) by true \(\theta\), the SD of the estimates and the group difference. It is base R and runs in a second.

With the seed as written, 112 of the 5,000 respondents (2.2%) have no finite MLE, and optimize() on a perfect score returns 10, its upper bound. The MLE and WLE are close to unbiased across the middle; the EAP and MAP are pulled toward 0, by about 0.16 within one SD of the mean, 0.5 one to two SDs out, and 1 beyond. In return the EAP’s RMSE is 0.52 in the middle, against 0.78 to 0.80 for the MLE, and 0.61 overall against 0.79 (without the 112). At the ends the order reverses. The SDs are 1.26 (MLE) and 0.80 (EAP) against a true 1, and the group difference is 0.98 (MLE), 1.03 (WLE) and 0.65 (EAP). With length.out at 20 or 40, the EAP’s group difference rises to 0.80 and 0.89.

Which estimate to report

The choice rests on what we’ll assume and on what the numbers are for.

  • The MLE and WLE assume nothing about the population: the estimate comes from the respondent’s own responses, which suits comparisons across populations. The price is noise: on Wordsum the MLE’s standard errors run from 0.79 to 1.13, and 12% of respondents have no MLE.
  • The EAP and MAP assume the respondent comes from the population the prior describes. If so, they are closer to the truth on average (in the simulation, an RMSE of 0.61 against 0.79). The price is shrinkage toward that population’s mean, largest at the ends and on short tests, which carries into spreads and group differences.

When I report a score to an individual from a test like this one, I report the EAP with its posterior SD, and I say that it is pulled toward the mean of the population the prior describes. It always exists, it is the most accurate on average for the people the test was given to, and its standard error grows where the items run out. What I don’t do is average EAPs to compare groups: the simulation’s group difference of 1 came back as 0.65. The MLEs and WLEs came closer, but they vary too much for spreads and correlations. If a prior is unwelcome, say because respondents come from different populations, I’d use the WLE rather than the MLE, which loses everyone at the floor and the ceiling. For group questions I’d put the groups into the model, or use plausible values. And if the Rasch model fits and everyone took the same items, The Rasch model has already argued for the sum score: every estimator in the table above is a relabelling of it.

Problems

  1. Three items by hand. For Rasch items with \(b = -1, 0, 1.5\), plot the likelihood of 1-1-0. At what \(\theta\) does 1-1-0 become more likely than 1-0-0, and why is that a difficulty? (PS4#1)
  2. The Rasch score equation. Show that the Rasch MLE solves \(\sum_i P_i(\theta) = r\). Then use the factorization from the Rasch lesson’s Go deeper callout to show that the EAP depends on the pattern only through \(r\) too.
  3. Change the prior. Rescore Wordsum by EAP with a prior SD of 1 instead of 1.88 (fscores(m, method = "EAP", mean = 0, cov = 1)). Which respondents move most? What happens to the SD of the EAPs, and why?
  4. Classroom averages. A school compares classroom means of students’ EAPs from a 15-item test, with one normal prior for everyone. What goes wrong, in which direction, and what would you report instead?
  5. Does EAP reduce error? Extend the simulation: 1,000 respondents at each true \(\theta\) from −3 to 3 in steps of 0.5. Where does the EAP’s RMSE beat the MLE’s, and where not? Then score normal(0, 1) respondents with a prior of mean 1. What changes? (PS5#2)
  6. Challenge (open): the floor and the ceiling. A survey analyst wants Wordsum as a predictor of, say, trust in science. What should they do with the 251 respondents at 0 or 10: drop them, use WLEs, EAPs or plausible values, or fit the regression inside the item response model? Does it depend on the question? I don’t think there is a single answer; make the case for yours.

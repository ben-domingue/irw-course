What this is for

What does a 3 mean on a scale from 1 (disagree) to 5 (agree)? The graded response model reads it as a little more of the trait than a 2. But a respondent may choose the middle because they have no view, or prefer not to commit, and a 5 may say as much about a taste for the ends of a scale as about the trait. An IRTree writes the response as a sequence of decisions (take a side? which side? how far?) and gives each its own item response model and, if we want, its own \(\theta\). Models for polytomous responses split a response into binary questions fixed by the model; a tree chooses the questions to match a process we have in mind, and fitting it is a recoding followed by a glmer() fit from What is an item?. On ten conscientiousness items, leaving the middle and going to the ends turn out to be largely one tendency, which barely tracks conscientiousness.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • write a rating as a path through a tree of binary nodes, and its probability as a product along the path;
  • choose a tree’s shape (linear or nested) and its abilities (one \(\theta\), or one per node), and say which choice gives the sequential model;
  • recode long response data into pseudo-items and fit a tree with glmer();
  • separate content from midpoint and extreme responding, and say what the separation assumes;
  • use a first node for speed or skipping, and say what it protects the content estimate from.

Core ideas

A response as a sequence of decisions

Recall from Models for polytomous responses: ordinal models split a response into binary questions. The sequential model (Tutz, 1990) asks “having reached category \(k-1\), does the respondent go on to \(k\)?”, and a response answers only the questions it reaches.

Nothing requires the questions to follow the order of the categories. Böckenholt (2012) proposed a tree for a five-point rating that asks three different questions. Does the respondent take a side or choose the middle? Which side? And do they go to its end? Each question is a node, and each node gets an item response model. With a Rasch model at every node, \[p^{(n)}_{ij} = \Pr(\text{node } n = 1) = \frac{\exp\big(\theta^{(n)}_j - b^{(n)}_i\big)}{1 + \exp\big(\theta^{(n)}_j - b^{(n)}_i\big)}, \qquad n \in \{\text{side}, \text{direction}, \text{extreme}\},\] and the probability of a response is the product of the node probabilities along its path. A 4 takes a side, the agree side, and stops short of the end: \[\Pr(x_{ij} = 4) = p^{(\text{side})}_{ij}\; p^{(\text{direction})}_{ij}\,\big(1 - p^{(\text{extreme})}_{ij}\big), \qquad \Pr(x_{ij} = 3) = 1 - p^{(\text{side})}_{ij}.\] Each response becomes one binary pseudo-item per node it reaches; a node it doesn’t reach is absent, not a 0 (De Boeck & Partchev, 2012).

Tree shape and node abilities are choices

Two choices define a tree. Its shape: a linear tree asks its questions in a chain, as the sequential model does; a nested tree branches, so that different responses pass through different nodes, as Böckenholt’s does (De Boeck & Partchev, 2012). And its abilities: one \(\theta\) shared by every node, or a correlated \(\theta\) per node. A linear tree with one \(\theta\) and Rasch steps is Tutz’s sequential model. Jeon and De Boeck (2016) extend trees further, with slopes at each node and more flexible links between nodes and dimensions.

Fitting a tree is recoding, then a mixed model

Recall from What is an item?: the Rasch model is a mixed logistic regression, glmer(resp ~ 0 + item + (1 | id), family = binomial), with one intercept per item (its easiness, \(-b_i\)) and a random intercept per respondent (\(\theta_j\)). Item features and respondent covariates are changes to that formula.

How does a rating become pseudo-items? Take three respondents from the conscientiousness data in With real data and their answers to the first two items, C1 and C2. (C2 is worded against conscientiousness, so its responses are reversed first, and higher means more conscientious on both.) Böckenholt’s tree can be written as a map with one row per category and one column per node. Each row is that category’s path, with NA where the path doesn’t reach a node, and recoding is looking each response up in the map:

A tree is one of those regressions. With the long rows as the data (pseudo_items),

glmer(resp ~ 0 + item:node + (0 + node | id), family = binomial, data = pseudo_items)

gives every item an easiness at every node (item:node) and every respondent a \(\theta\) at every node, with their SDs and correlations estimated ((0 + node | id)). The node is an item predictor, and it has to be a factor: stored as a number, glmer would treat it as one slope. One \(\theta\) for all nodes is (1 | id). Any software for binary item response models can fit a tree, because the likelihood factors into one piece per node (Go deeper, below); mirt (Chalmers, 2012) takes the pseudo-items as columns, missing where a response doesn’t reach a node.

NoteGo deeper: the tree likelihood factorizes over nodes

Assumptions: a tree with nodes \(n = 1, \dots, N\); each response category corresponds to exactly one path; at each node, the probability of a 1 depends only on the respondent’s node ability and the item’s node parameters; given the node abilities, the node outcomes are independent, within and across items.

One response. Write \(y_{ijn} \in \{0, 1\}\) for the pseudo-item of respondent \(j\), item \(i\) and node \(n\), and \(r_{ijn} = 1\) if the response reaches node \(n\) (0 otherwise). The path of \(x_{ij}\) fixes both, so \[\Pr(x_{ij} \mid \boldsymbol\theta_j) = \prod_{n} \Big[\big(p^{(n)}_{ij}\big)^{y_{ijn}} \big(1 - p^{(n)}_{ij}\big)^{1 - y_{ijn}}\Big]^{r_{ijn}},\] where a node the response doesn’t reach contributes a factor of 1.

All responses. Multiplying over items and respondents, \[L = \prod_j \int \prod_n \prod_{i:\, r_{ijn} = 1} \big(p^{(n)}_{ij}\big)^{y_{ijn}} \big(1 - p^{(n)}_{ij}\big)^{1 - y_{ijn}}\; \phi(\boldsymbol\theta_j; \mathbf 0, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\, d\boldsymbol\theta_j.\] Given \(\boldsymbol\theta_j\), this is the likelihood of an ordinary binary item response model for a data set with one column per item and node, and missing entries where \(r_{ijn} = 0\). Whether a node is reached is fixed by the outcomes of earlier nodes, which are observed, so the missing pseudo-items are missing at random in the sense of Rubin (1976), and leaving them out loses nothing. That is why recoding followed by glmer() or mirt fits the tree, with no special software.

Two consequences. If \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) is diagonal (node abilities uncorrelated), the integral splits and the tree is \(N\) separate item response models, one per node. And if all nodes share one \(\theta\) and the tree is linear with Rasch nodes, the product is the sequential model of Models for polytomous responses, whose steps Tutz (1990) fits the same way.

Response styles

If you’ve done From construct map to items: acquiescence, agreeing whatever the statement, is one reason scales include reversed items. Once the reversed items are keyed, acquiescence pulls the two halves of the sum in opposite directions and largely cancels.

Acquiescence is one response style, a tendency in how a respondent uses the scale. Two others are midpoint responding, choosing the middle, and extreme responding, choosing the ends (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001). Reversed items don’t balance these two, since reversing maps 5 to 1 and 3 to 3. In Böckenholt’s tree they have their own nodes. The \(\theta\) at the side node is a respondent’s tendency to leave the middle; the \(\theta\) at the extreme node is their tendency to go to the ends; and the content \(\theta\), at the direction node, is estimated net of both (Böckenholt, 2017). Khorramdel and von Davier (2014) fit style dimensions shared across all five Big Five scales.

The separation rests on assumptions. The tree takes a 3 to carry no information about direction, and takes the side and extreme decisions to be related to content only through the correlations of the \(\theta\)s. Plieninger and Meiser (2014) checked the style dimensions against external measures of response style and found that they behaved as style; Meiser, Plieninger and Henninger (2019) relax the first assumption with nodes that are themselves ordinal.

Trees for what the response hides: speed and skips

If you’ve done Guessing and priors: in a timed lexical decision task, responses under 0.3 seconds were correct at about chance, and a few respondents gave most of them. Rapid guessing there looked like a property of respondents, not items. Skipped responses were left for this lesson.

A first node can record what the response doesn’t show. A node for “answered in under \(t\) seconds” gives every respondent a speed \(\theta\), correlated with the content \(\theta\), and the content node can then be fitted to slow responses only, or given separate item parameters for fast and slow ones (Partchev & De Boeck, 2012; DiTrapani, Jeon, De Boeck & Partchev, 2016). Time as a continuous variable is the subject of Response time and the speed-accuracy tradeoff.

A skip is usually scored as wrong or left missing, both reasonable when skipping has nothing to do with ability. When lower-ability respondents skip more, skipping carries information about \(\theta\), and the missing responses are not ignorable (Holman & Glas, 2005; Glas & Pimentel, 2008). A tree whose first node is “answered?” and second “correct, given answered?” puts that information into the model: the correlation between the two node \(\theta\)s carries it to the content estimate (Debeer, Janssen & De Boeck, 2017). None of the IRW tables with a tokenless download has enough skips to show this, so the simulation below does.

Simulate

Part 1 generates 1–5 ratings from Böckenholt’s tree (400 respondents, 10 items, correlated node \(\theta\)s), fits one \(\theta\) and a \(\theta\) per node, and compares both with the truth. Part 2 simulates a 15-item right–wrong test on which lower-ability respondents skip more, and scores the skips as wrong, as missing, and with a skip node. The first run takes one to two minutes, most of it installing and loading lme4; nAGQ = 0 keeps each fit to seconds.

With the seed as written, AIC prefers a \(\theta\) per node (11,234 against 11,679), and the node SDs and correlations come back within about 0.13 of the truth. The content estimates from the per-node tree correlate 0.88 with the true content \(\theta\), against 0.69 from the one-\(\theta\) tree, which mixes content with style. In part 2, the 77 respondents who skipped most skipped about half the items. Scored as wrong, their abilities come out 0.24 too low on average; left missing, 0.41 too high, since the model pulls respondents with few answers toward the mean, and these respondents are below it. With the skip node the error is 0.02. Try R with every correlation set to 0, a skip rate that doesn’t depend on ability (skipper <- rnorm(np2)), or np <- 2000 locally.

With real data

Conscientiousness and response styles

The main example is bfi_goldberg_1992_conscientiousness: the ten conscientiousness items of the IPIP Big-Five factor markers (Goldberg, 1992; Goldberg et al., 2006), answered online around 2012 through the Open-Source Psychometrics Project. Its job is to be a five-point scale on which response styles could be at work. The IRW’s item-text check confirmed that these are the IPIP markers rather than the Big Five Inventory. IPIP items are in the public domain, so we can quote them: C1 is “I am always prepared” and C2 “I leave my belongings around” (Goldberg et al., 2006; IPIP key). Responses run from 1 (disagree) through 3 (neutral) to 5 (agree), with 2 and 4 unlabelled. Download the code to run every chunk yourself.

Recall from Classical test theory and reliability: items worded against the construct have to be reverse-keyed before they are combined, and the key should come from the documentation and be checked in the data, since neither is enough alone.

In a tree, keying decides what the direction node means: keyed, it measures conscientiousness on every item; unkeyed, it asks “agree?”, which mixes conscientiousness with acquiescence. The side and extreme nodes don’t change. The IPIP key reverses C2, C4, C6 and C8, and the data agree:

Can the tree’s AIC be compared with the graded response model’s? Only when both are computed the same way from the same responses. glmer with nAGQ = 0 approximates the marginal likelihood and mirt integrates it by quadrature, so the same tree gets a different log likelihood from each; and a tree that drops categories, as the speed tree below drops the content of fast responses, describes different data. Böckenholt’s tree describes all five categories, so we fit it and the GRM in mirt:

Code 
# Can the tree's AIC be compared with the GRM's? Fit both by the same method: mirt,
# which integrates over theta by quadrature. The tree becomes 30 pseudo-items (NA
# where a response doesn't reach a node), each loading on its node's dimension with
# a slope fixed at 1; the three dimensions have free variances and correlations.
pseudo <- data.frame(ifelse(x == 3, 0, 1),
                     ifelse(x == 3, NA, 1 * (x > 3)),
                     ifelse(x == 3, NA, 1 * (x == 1 | x == 5)))
names(pseudo) <- paste0(rep(c("side_", "dir_", "ext_"), each = 10), colnames(x))
spec <- mirt::mirt.model("side = 1-10\n dir = 11-20\n ext = 21-30\n COV = side*dir*ext")
tree_mirt <- mirt::mirt(pseudo, spec, itemtype = "Rasch", verbose = FALSE)
tree_mirt1 <- mirt::mirt(pseudo, 1, itemtype = "Rasch", verbose = FALSE)  # one theta
grm <- mirt::mirt(as.data.frame(x), 1, itemtype = "graded", verbose = FALSE)
fits <- list(tree_mirt1, tree_mirt, grm)
data.frame(parameters = c(attr(logLik(one), "df"), attr(logLik(per), "df"),
                          sapply(fits, mirt::extract.mirt, "nest")),
           logLik = round(c(logLik(one), logLik(per), sapply(fits, mirt::extract.mirt, "logLik"))),
           AIC = round(c(AIC(one), AIC(per), sapply(fits, mirt::extract.mirt, "AIC"))),
           row.names = c("tree, one theta, glmer", "tree, theta per node, glmer",
                         "tree, one theta, mirt", "tree, theta per node, mirt", "GRM, mirt"))

If you’ve done Model fit and out-of-sample prediction: AIC compares models fitted to the same data by the same likelihood; it says which model is preferred, not how much better it predicts. The IMV puts that on a common scale.

The per-node tree’s log likelihood is \(-41{,}263\) from glmer and \(-41{,}144\) from mirt: 119 apart from the approximation alone. Within mirt, that tree beats the GRM (AIC 82,361 against 83,242, with 36 parameters against 50), and the tree with one \(\theta\) falls far behind both (85,699). The tree wins because it has style dimensions the GRM lacks (problem 3 gives a rival the same advantage).

What does the choice rest on? An assumption about the response process: that choosing the middle, a side and the end are separate decisions. When such a process is in view (midpoint avoidance, extreme responding, speed, skipping), the tree gives it a parameter and estimates the content net of it; the one-\(\theta\) tree, which drops that, was the worst of the three here. AIC says which model is preferred, not how much better each predicts. Comparing the trees and their rivals on held-out responses with the IMV (Model fit and out-of-sample prediction) puts that on a common scale, and it is a comparison you can run for any set of trees on any rating scale.

Extraversion and fast responses

For contrast, introversion_extroversion: 91 statements from the development of the Multidimensional Introversion-Extraversion Scales, answered online around 2012 through the same project (MIES development data and codebook), with the time spent on each. Its job is to ask whether fast responding says anything about the trait. Items 81–85 and 89–91 are eight of the ten IPIP extraversion markers (Goldberg, 1992), the same family as the conscientiousness items, and we use those. Responses run from 1 (disagree) to 5 (agree). Five of the eight are worded toward introversion (“I keep in the background”, for one; MIES codebook), so we key them, higher meaning more extraverted.

Problems

  1. The tree’s probabilities. For Böckenholt’s tree, write \(\Pr(x = k)\) for \(k = 1, \dots, 5\) in terms of the three node probabilities, and show that they sum to 1. Then show that a linear tree with Rasch nodes and one \(\theta\) is the sequential model, and explain why it is not the partial credit model, although both have one parameter per category boundary.
  2. The other four traits. Fit Böckenholt’s tree to the other four bfi_goldberg_1992_* tables (key each from the IPIP key, checked in the data). Are the side and extreme \(\theta\)s as strongly correlated for every trait? Do one respondent’s style \(\theta\)s from different traits correlate, as a style should? (The five tables share respondent ids.)
  3. Comparing the tree with the GRM. When can you compare the tree’s AIC with the GRM’s? Then give a rival without a tree the same advantage: in mirt, a multidimensional GPCM whose second dimension scores categories 1 and 5 as 1 and the others as 0 (the gpcm_mats argument) has an extreme-response dimension of its own. Compare it with the tree. What does the comparison say about the tree’s advantage over the GRM?
  4. Two countries. A survey is given in two countries whose respondents differ in how often they choose the ends of a scale. What can Böckenholt’s tree correct for when you compare the countries’ means, and what can’t it? Would you change the instrument as well as the model?
  5. The 2-second cut. Refit the speed tree with cuts at 1, 1.5 and 3 seconds. How does the share of fast responses change, and the correlation between speed and extraversion? Is there a cut below which fast responses stop tracking the trait? (PS8#4)
  6. Challenge (open): the skip \(\theta\). On a low-stakes test, a skip node gives every respondent a second \(\theta\), their tendency to answer. Does it belong in the reported score? Consider what the score is for and whether a respondent could raise it by skipping less. I don’t have a general answer.

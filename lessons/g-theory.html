What this is for

Classical test theory gives a score one error term. Suppose 202 people list unusual uses for a paperclip, a garbage bag and a rope, and five raters judge how clever each list is. A respondent might have done better with other objects, and would have got a different score from other raters. Which source of error is larger decides whether to add raters or objects. Generalizability theory (G theory) asks what the score should generalize over, then splits the error into those sources and prices each one (Cronbach, Rajaratnam & Gleser, 1963). In the cleverness ratings the answer is more tasks; in teachers’ ratings of essays, more raters. Like CTT, G theory is a theory of observed scores. Item response theory, from The Rasch model on, is a latent-trait theory that models each response, and the two rest on different assumptions.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • name the facets of a measurement design, say which are crossed and which nested, and decide which the score should generalize over (random) and which not (fixed);
  • estimate variance components for a persons × tasks × raters design with lme4, and say which ones are error for which decision;
  • compute relative and absolute G coefficients, and show that the one-facet relative coefficient is alpha;
  • use a D study to decide whether to add tasks or raters;
  • compute consistency and agreement intraclass correlations, and say when each is the one to report.

Core ideas

One error term, many sources

Recall from Classical test theory and reliability: an observed score is \(X = T + E\), reliability is \(\sigma^2_T/(\sigma^2_T + \sigma^2_E)\), alpha estimates it from the items of one administration, and the Spearman–Brown formula says what lengthening the test does.

Where does \(E\) come from? The items behind alpha are one source; From construct map to items added another, the rater who scores a constructed response; occasions and forms are more. G theory calls each such source a facet, and each of its values (this rater, that task) a condition (Cronbach, Gleser, Nanda & Rajaratnam, 1972, a book with no DOI, linked here through Bock’s review in Science).

Recall from Constructs and construct maps: a test built from a blueprint supports a claim about a domain, and its items can be seen as a sample from a larger pool.

G theory applies that view to every facet. The tasks we used are a sample from the tasks we could have used; the raters, from the raters we would accept. Together these make up the universe of admissible observations, which the investigator, not the data, defines. A decision maker may care about only part of it (raters trained on this rubric, say); that part is the universe of generalization. A respondent’s average over it is the universe score, G theory’s counterpart of the true score, and the question of this lesson is how well an average over the few conditions we used stands in for it (Shavelson, Webb & Rowley, 1989). A facet we generalize over is random; a facet we don’t (these three objects and no others) is fixed, and what is specific to its conditions stops being error (Brennan, 2001).

Variance components

Take the cleverness design: every person \(p\) does every task \(t\), and every rater \(r\) rates every response, so all three are crossed. A rating is a sum of effects: \[X_{ptr} = \mu + \nu_p + \nu_t + \nu_r + \nu_{pt} + \nu_{pr} + \nu_{tr} + \nu_{ptr,e}.\] Each \(\nu\) is a random effect with mean zero and its own variance, and the effects are uncorrelated, so the variance of one rating splits into seven variance components, \(\sigma^2_p + \sigma^2_t + \dots + \sigma^2_{ptr,e}\) (Brennan, 2001). \(\sigma^2_p\) is how much people differ, the signal; \(\sigma^2_r\), how much raters differ in severity; \(\sigma^2_{pt}\), how much people’s standing changes from task to task; \(\sigma^2_{pr}\), raters disagreeing about who is better. The last term mixes the three-way interaction with whatever else the design can’t separate from it, hence the \(e\).

Not every design is crossed. If each essay is read by its own teachers, raters are nested within essays (\(r{:}p\)), and a teacher seen with one essay can’t show both how harsh they are and how they react to that essay: \(\sigma^2_r\) and \(\sigma^2_{pr}\) merge into \(\sigma^2_{r:p}\) (Brennan, 2001).

How do we estimate them? Classically, by setting the mean squares of an analysis of variance equal to their expected values, a reasonable choice for a balanced design (Brennan, 2001) and one the essay section uses as a check. This lesson fits each term as a random intercept in a mixed model, with lme4 (Bates, Mächler, Bolker & Walker, 2015) and a long table of one row per rating:

lmer(resp ~ 1 + (1 | id) + (1 | item) + (1 | rater) +
       (1 | id:item) + (1 | id:rater) + (1 | item:rater), data = ratings)

The residual variance is \(\sigma^2_{ptr,e}\). lmer estimates the variances by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Plain maximum likelihood treats the fixed effects as known and so underestimates each variance, by more the fewer levels a facet has; REML removes that bias (Patterson & Thompson, 1971; Harville, 1977), which matters with facets of three and five levels. In a balanced design REML reproduces the ANOVA estimates whenever none is negative (Searle, Casella & McCulloch, 1992), and it also handles sparse designs like the second table’s.

Two G coefficients

Why are some components error only some of the time? It depends on the decision. For a relative decision (who ranks above whom), only the components that change a person’s standing count: those that interact with \(p\). For a score averaged over \(n_t\) tasks and \(n_r\) raters, the relative error variance is \[\sigma^2_\delta = \frac{\sigma^2_{pt}}{n_t} + \frac{\sigma^2_{pr}}{n_r} + \frac{\sigma^2_{ptr,e}}{n_t n_r}.\] For an absolute decision (is this score above 3? how clever is this list, on the rating scale?), a harsh rater or a hard task moves the score itself, so the main effects count too: \[\sigma^2_\Delta = \sigma^2_\delta + \frac{\sigma^2_t}{n_t} + \frac{\sigma^2_r}{n_r} + \frac{\sigma^2_{tr}}{n_t n_r}.\] Each coefficient sets the universe-score variance against one of them (Brennan, 2001): the generalizability coefficient \(E\rho^2 = \sigma^2_p/(\sigma^2_p + \sigma^2_\delta)\) and the dependability coefficient \(\Phi = \sigma^2_p/(\sigma^2_p + \sigma^2_\Delta)\). Since \(\sigma^2_\Delta \ge \sigma^2_\delta\), \(\Phi\) is never the larger. Error near a cut score is taken up in What does a score mean?.

Both have the form of reliability in Classical test theory and reliability, \(\sigma^2_T/(\sigma^2_T + \sigma^2_E)\): the “true” variance is universe-score variance, \(\sigma^2_p\), what stays with a person across the conditions we generalize over, and the error is what changes from one sample of conditions to another. Fix a facet and the split moves (the interrater section shows how).

What does the simplest case look like? Keep one facet, items, in a persons × items design. Then \(\sigma^2_\delta = \sigma^2_{pi,e}/n_i\) and \[E\rho^2 = \frac{\sigma^2_p}{\sigma^2_p + \sigma^2_{pi,e}/n_i},\] which is Cronbach’s alpha (Cronbach, 1951; Hoyt, 1941, estimated reliability from the same analysis of variance). The callout shows why.

NoteGo deeper: why the one-facet coefficient is alpha

Assumptions: a persons × items design with \(n_p\) persons and \(n_i\) items, one score per cell, \(X_{pi} = \mu + \nu_p + \nu_i + \nu_{pi,e}\), with uncorrelated random effects (Brennan, 2001).

In a two-way analysis of variance without replication, \(E(MS_p) = \sigma^2_{pi,e} + n_i\sigma^2_p\) and \(E(MS_{pi,e}) = \sigma^2_{pi,e}\), so \(\hat\sigma^2_p = (MS_p - MS_{pi,e})/n_i\), \(\hat\sigma^2_{pi,e} = MS_{pi,e}\), and \[\widehat{E\rho^2} = \frac{\hat\sigma^2_p}{\hat\sigma^2_p + \hat\sigma^2_{pi,e}/n_i} = 1 - \frac{MS_{pi,e}}{MS_p}.\]

Why it is alpha. Write \(X_p = \sum_i X_{pi}\) for a person’s total and \(X_{\cdot i}\) for item \(i\)’s column, and use sample variances with divisor \(n_p - 1\). The persons sum of squares is \(n_i\) times the sum of squared deviations of person means, so \(MS_p = \text{Var}(X_p)/n_i\). The residual sum of squares is what is left of the total after the person and item parts, \((n_p - 1)\big[\sum_i \text{Var}(X_{\cdot i}) - \text{Var}(X_p)/n_i\big]\), on \((n_p-1)(n_i-1)\) degrees of freedom. So \[\frac{MS_{pi,e}}{MS_p} = \frac{n_i\sum_i \text{Var}(X_{\cdot i}) - \text{Var}(X_p)}{(n_i - 1)\,\text{Var}(X_p)},\] and \[1 - \frac{MS_{pi,e}}{MS_p} = \frac{n_i}{n_i - 1}\left(1 - \frac{\sum_i \text{Var}(X_{\cdot i})}{\text{Var}(X_p)}\right) = \alpha. \qquad\blacksquare\] The equality holds exactly in any sample, not only in expectation. Problem 1 asks you to fill in the sums of squares.

D studies: what would another design buy?

A decision study (D study) uses the G study’s components to ask what a different design would give for the universe of generalization: which facets are random, and how many conditions of each are averaged over. Change \(n_t\) and \(n_r\) in the formulas and recompute. With one facet this is Spearman–Brown (problem 1); with two, the components say which lever moves the coefficient more.

What are we assuming when a D study adds raters we never had, ten when the data had five? That new raters are exchangeable draws from the universe of generalization, disagreeing with the others as much as the old ones did, and that the design stays crossed. Raters from a different pool change \(\sigma^2_r\) and \(\sigma^2_{pr}\); if each respondent gets different raters, severity joins the relative error (next section). And components estimated from a handful of raters make a far projection uncertain (Simulate shows how noisy a facet with three levels is).

Interrater reliability as a G study

In a persons × raters design, the setting for interrater reliability, the intraclass correlations of Shrout and Fleiss (1979) and McGraw and Wong (1996) are G coefficients. The consistency ICC for one rater, ICC(C,1) in McGraw and Wong’s notation, is \(\sigma^2_p/(\sigma^2_p + \sigma^2_{pr,e})\), the relative coefficient. The absolute agreement ICC, ICC(A,1), is \(\sigma^2_p/(\sigma^2_p + \sigma^2_r + \sigma^2_{pr,e})\), the dependability coefficient. Averaged over \(k\) raters, they are the D study for \(k\) raters.

When raters score the same tasks and the question is only whether another rater would score the same response the same way, the tasks are fixed. Brennan (2001) then puts \(\sigma^2_{pt}\) in the universe score: for one rater on one task the coefficient is \((\sigma^2_p + \sigma^2_{pt})/(\sigma^2_p + \sigma^2_{pt} + \sigma^2_{pr} + \sigma^2_{ptr,e})\), the answer to a narrower question than a coefficient that generalizes over tasks too.

Which should you report? If every respondent has the same raters and only the order matters, severity cancels. If scores are compared with a fixed standard, or respondents have different raters (each essay its own teachers, a nested design), a harsh rater lowers some scores and not others, and rater variance is error even for ranking (Brennan, 2001). In the case that raters vary across respondents or a cut is involved, the absolute coefficient, \(\Phi\) or the agreement ICC, may be the one to report; for a fully crossed design whose scores are used only to rank, the consistency coefficient may be sufficient for some purposes.

Code 
library(lme4)
options(digits = 7, scipen = 5)  # R's default digits, in case a .Rprofile changes it; no e-notation
# Variance components from a crossed random-effects fit, with their shares of the
# total. `labels` renames lme4's grouping factors (id, item, rater and their
# interactions) to the facets of the design.
components <- function(fit, labels) {
  vc <- as.data.frame(VarCorr(fit))
  v <- setNames(vc$vcov, labels[vc$grp])[labels]
  data.frame(variance = round(v, 3), percent = round(100 * v / sum(v), 1))
}
# G coefficients for a D study that averages over n_i levels of the first facet
# (tasks, or criteria) and n_r raters. v holds the seven components in the order
# p, i, r, pi, pr, ir, residual.
g_coef <- function(v, n_i, n_r) {
  rel <- v[4] / n_i + v[5] / n_r + v[7] / (n_i * n_r)
  abs <- rel + v[2] / n_i + v[3] / n_r + v[6] / (n_i * n_r)
  c(relative = v[1] / (v[1] + rel), absolute = v[1] / (v[1] + abs))
}
labels <- c(id = "person", item = "task", rater = "rater", "id:item" = "person x task",
            "id:rater" = "person x rater", "item:rater" = "task x rater",
            Residual = "residual (p x t x r, e)")
form <- resp ~ 1 + (1 | id) + (1 | item) + (1 | rater) +
  (1 | id:item) + (1 | id:rater) + (1 | item:rater)

The design as run, 3 tasks and 5 raters, has a relative G coefficient of 0.71. One rater gives 0.56, but one rater with 6 tasks gives 0.68: 6 ratings per respondent instead of 15, for nearly the same coefficient. No number of raters takes 3 tasks past 0.77, the ceiling set by person × task. For this construct and these raters, the money goes on tasks. The absolute coefficient equals the relative one to two decimals (0.71), since the task and rater components are small.

A second rater would score the same list much as the first did (0.64), but a respondent’s cleverness with one object says much less about their cleverness in general (0.33). Same ratings, two universes.

Essays: when raters matter

The contrast is teacherjudgements_lohmann_2026_essayratings, from a study in which preservice English teachers rated essays by learners of English as a second language (Lohmann et al., 2026; CC BY 4.0). Each teacher rated five essays on four criteria (language quality, structure, content, and a holistic rating), from 1 (very low quality) to 7 (very high quality). “Person” in the output now means essay, and criteria take the place of tasks. This design is sparse. Every teacher rated 5 essays, but 435 of the 881 essays were rated by a single teacher, and only 50 by four or more. Each essay has its own teachers, so raters are effectively nested within essays, though each teacher’s five essays link the teachers to one another.

How does the fit handle the essay–teacher pairs never observed? Nothing is filled in. REML uses only the ratings that exist and learns each component where ratings overlap: a teacher’s severity from how their five essays fare against other ratings of those essays, essay × rater from essays that two or more teachers read, essay × criterion from the four criteria of every rating. An essay read by one teacher informs the essay and criterion components but can’t, alone, separate that teacher’s view of it from the essay itself.

Treating each essay’s teachers as its own gives the design \((r{:}p) \times t\) (criteria as \(t\)), with five components: essay, criterion, rater within essay, essay × criterion and the residual. The classic route works here: compute the mean squares and solve the expected-mean-squares equations (the callout gives them).

Code 
# The classic route, by hand: sums of squares, mean squares, then the
# expected-mean-squares equations solved for the five components (see the callout).
# ave() returns each row's group mean, so the sums run over all ratings.
n_p <- length(unique(bal$id)); n_r <- 3; n_c <- 4
m    <- mean(bal$resp)
m_p  <- ave(bal$resp, bal$id)                 # essay means
m_c  <- ave(bal$resp, bal$item)               # criterion means
m_rp <- ave(bal$resp, bal$id, bal$rater)      # teacher-within-essay means
m_pc <- ave(bal$resp, bal$id, bal$item)       # essay x criterion means

        pc = sum((m_pc - m_p - m_c + m)^2), "rc:p,e" = sum((bal$resp - m_rp - m_pc + m_p)^2))

NoteGo deeper: expected mean squares for raters nested within essays

Assumptions: \(n_p\) essays, each rated by its own \(n_r\) raters on the same \(n_t\) criteria, one rating per cell, \(X_{prt} = \mu + \nu_p + \nu_t + \nu_{r:p} + \nu_{pt} + \nu_{rt:p,e}\), with uncorrelated random effects (Brennan, 2001). Here \(\sigma^2_{r:p} = \sigma^2_r + \sigma^2_{pr}\) and \(\sigma^2_{rt:p,e} = \sigma^2_{rt} + \sigma^2_{prt,e}\) from the crossed design.

\[\begin{aligned} E(MS_p) &= \sigma^2_{rt:p,e} + n_t\sigma^2_{r:p} + n_r\sigma^2_{pt} + n_r n_t\sigma^2_p\\ E(MS_t) &= \sigma^2_{rt:p,e} + n_r\sigma^2_{pt} + n_p n_r\sigma^2_t\\ E(MS_{r:p}) &= \sigma^2_{rt:p,e} + n_t\sigma^2_{r:p}\\ E(MS_{pt}) &= \sigma^2_{rt:p,e} + n_r\sigma^2_{pt}\\ E(MS_{rt:p,e}) &= \sigma^2_{rt:p,e} \end{aligned}\]

Set each mean square equal to its expectation and solve from the bottom up: \(\hat\sigma^2_{rt:p,e} = MS_{rt:p,e}\), \(\hat\sigma^2_{pt} = (MS_{pt} - MS_{rt:p,e})/n_r\), \(\hat\sigma^2_{r:p} = (MS_{r:p} - MS_{rt:p,e})/n_t\), \(\hat\sigma^2_t = (MS_t - MS_{pt})/(n_p n_r)\), and \(\hat\sigma^2_p = (MS_p - MS_{r:p} - MS_{pt} + MS_{rt:p,e})/(n_r n_t)\). These are the lines of v_anova, which writes c for criteria. Some teachers read more than one of these essays; the nested analysis ignores that and treats each essay’s three as its own.

Now the same design by REML, next to the full sparse fit with its components combined to match (rater within essay is rater plus essay × rater; the residual absorbs criterion × rater):

ANOVA and REML agree to the third decimal: in a balanced design with no negative estimate, REML is the ANOVA solution. The full sparse fit is close: essay 0.754 against 0.775, rater within essay 0.886 against 0.902, residual 0.648 against 0.597. The gaps have three sources: a different set of essays (126 of 881, picked by how many teachers they got); a small sample, so noisy estimates (criterion, from four levels, most of all: 0.010 against 0.023); and a design that can’t split rater within essay into severity and essay × rater, as the full fit can. Where the data allow the classical estimate, REML on the sparse design gives much the same answer.

The two tables call for different designs, and only their variance components could say so.

Problems

  1. Derivation. Fill in the sums of squares in the Go deeper callout to show that \(1 - MS_{pi,e}/MS_p\) is alpha. Then show that the D-study coefficient for \(m n_i\) items is the Spearman–Brown formula with multiplier \(m\).
  2. Real data with a twist. From the cleverness components, find the combinations of tasks and raters that give a relative G coefficient of at least 0.8. Is there more than one? If a task costs a respondent five minutes and a rating costs a rater one minute per respondent, which design is cheapest for 200 respondents? Does the answer change for \(\Phi\)?
  3. Judgment: a sparse design. Refit the essay model using only essays with at least two teachers. Which components change, and how do they compare with the balanced subset’s? Suppose every essay had one teacher: which components could the data no longer separate, and what would that do to your D study?
  4. Design. Plan a G study for a performance assessment you know. What should the score generalize over? Name the facets, random or fixed, crossed or nested, and choose sample sizes. Which component do you expect to be largest?
  5. Real data: two raters. In the cleverness data, take raters 1 and 2 and the paperclip task. Compute the consistency and agreement ICCs from a lmer fit with random intercepts for respondent and rater. Which pair of raters agrees least?
  6. Challenge (open). G theory treats a harsh rater as a random effect to be averaged away. When should severity be estimated and adjusted for instead, and what would you need to know about the essays’ teachers to decide? (Rater models takes this up with the many-facet Rasch model.)

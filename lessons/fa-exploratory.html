What this is for

How many things does a set of items measure? Classical test theory mostly assumes the answer is one, and a high alpha doesn’t check that assumption. Many instruments are built to measure several things at once: a personality inventory with five domains, a quality-of-life scale with physical and emotional subscales. And an instrument meant to measure one thing may not, as the reverse-worded items in the instrument-building lesson showed. Factor analysis is the family of tools for asking what, and how many, underlying dimensions account for the correlations among a set of items. It is one of the oldest ideas in psychometrics. Spearman (1904) proposed it to explain why children who did well in one school subject tended to do well in others, and it remains the workhorse for studying the internal structure of a scale.

Recall from From construct map to items: reverse-worded items guard against acquiescence, but they can cluster together by the direction they’re worded in, so a scale written for one construct can show two dimensions.

This lesson is about the exploratory version: we let the data suggest a structure. Factor analysis II takes up the confirmatory version, where we state a structure in advance and test it; it also asks whether a structure holds across groups, and shows that factor analysis and item response theory are closer relatives than they first appear.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • write down the common factor model and explain how it reproduces a correlation matrix;
  • decide how many factors to extract, using eigenvalues and parallel analysis, and explain why common rules of thumb disagree;
  • explain what rotation does (and what it does not do) and choose between orthogonal and oblique rotations;
  • say why Likert items call for polychoric correlations, and how factor analysis differs from principal components;
  • run an exploratory factor analysis on real item responses and read the output.

Core ideas

A model for correlations

The data for factor analysis is a correlation matrix. The common factor model says that each standardized item \(x_i\) is a weighted combination of a small number of common factors plus something unique to that item: \[x_i = \lambda_{i1} f_1 + \lambda_{i2} f_2 + \dots + \lambda_{im} f_m + u_i.\] The weights \(\lambda\) are the loadings. The unique part \(u_i\) bundles measurement error with anything specific to the item, and it is assumed to be uncorrelated with the factors and with the other items’ unique parts. That last assumption carries the model: it says that everything the items have in common runs through the factors. Spearman’s version had one factor; Thurstone (1931) extended it to several, and his Multiple-factor analysis (Thurstone, 1947; see the review by Cattell, 1948) set out most of what follows.

With a single factor, the model makes a strong prediction. The correlation between items \(i\) and \(j\) is just the product of their loadings, \[\text{cor}(x_i, x_j) = \lambda_i \lambda_j,\] so an entire matrix of correlations is generated by one loading per item. Try it.

You should see two things. The item with the weakest loading has the weakest correlations with everything. And the pattern is rigid: the ratio of item 1’s correlations with items 3 and 4 is the same as the ratio of item 2’s. If real data didn’t have that rigid pattern, one factor would not be enough, which is how factor analysis detects that a second dimension is needed. Spearman turned the rigid pattern into a test, the vanishing “tetrad differences” (Spearman & Holzinger, 1924; problem 1).

A useful quantity falls out of this: an item’s communality, \(h_i^2 = \sum_k \lambda_{ik}^2\), is the share of its variance explained by the common factors. The remainder, \(1 - h_i^2\), is its uniqueness. An item with loading 0.3 on a single factor has a communality of \(0.3^2 = 0.09\); more than 90% of what it does is its own business.

A one-factor model is what “the items are unidimensional” means in factor-analytic terms, and it is a claim the data can reject.

Recall from Classical test theory and reliability: alpha is not a measure of unidimensionality, and it equals reliability only when the items are tau-equivalent. In the language of this lesson, tau-equivalence is a one-factor model with equal loadings (problem 2).

How many factors?

This is the question exploratory factor analysis is most often used to answer, and it has no single correct procedure. The usual starting point is the eigenvalues of the correlation matrix. Loosely, each eigenvalue says how much of the total variance (which, for \(p\) standardized items, is \(p\)) a dimension can account for, taken in decreasing order. A few large eigenvalues followed by a flat tail suggests a few factors.

Two classic rules of thumb read this list. The Kaiser rule keeps every factor with an eigenvalue above 1 (Guttman, 1954; Kaiser, 1960). The scree plot looks for the “elbow” where the eigenvalues level off (Cattell, 1966). Both have a problem: even data with no factor structure produce eigenvalues above 1, purely from sampling variability, and more so in small samples. Parallel analysis (Horn, 1965) fixes this by comparing the observed eigenvalues with those from random data of the same size, and keeping factors only while the observed ones are larger. Among the simple methods, it is the one I would reach for first.

viewof pa_l = Inputs.range([0.2, 0.8], {value: 0.5, step: 0.05, label: "Loading of each item"})
viewof pa_n = Inputs.range([50, 1000], {value: 150, step: 50, label: "Number of people"})
Code 
pa = {
  const p = 12, r = rng(21), sim = (loaded) => Array.from({length: pa_n}, () => {
    const f = Array.from({length: pa_k}, () => r.norm());
    return Array.from({length: p}, (_, j) => loaded ? pa_l * f[j % pa_k] + Math.sqrt(1 - pa_l ** 2) * r.norm() : r.norm());
  });
  const obs = eigenSym(corMatrix(sim(true)));
  const reps = 15, rand = Array(p).fill(0);
  for (let t = 0; t < reps; t++) eigenSym(corMatrix(sim(false))).forEach((v, i) => rand[i] += v / reps);
  let nPA = 0; while (nPA < p && obs[nPA] > rand[nPA]) nPA++;
  return {obs, rand, nPA, nKaiser: obs.filter(v => v > 1).length};
}

Rotation

Once we have more than one factor, which set of loadings is the right one? The data cannot say. With two or more factors the loadings are not unique: any rotation of the factors reproduces the correlation matrix exactly as well as any other. What rotation does is pick, from this infinite family of equally good solutions, one that is easy to interpret, ideally one with simple structure (Thurstone, 1947), where each item loads strongly on one factor and near zero on the rest.

Code 
rot = {
  const L0 = [[0.8, 0.1], [0.7, 0.0], [0.6, 0.15], [0.1, 0.75], [0.0, 0.7], [0.2, 0.6]];
  const a = rot_a * Math.PI / 180, c = Math.cos(a), s = Math.sin(a);
  const L = L0.map(([x, y]) => [c * x - s * y, s * x + c * y]);
  const impl = M => M.map(r => M.map(q => r[0] * q[0] + r[1] * q[1]));
  const d = Math.max(...impl(L).flat().map((v, i) => Math.abs(v - impl(L0).flat()[i])));
  const simple = L.reduce((u, [x, y]) => u + Math.abs(Math.abs(x) - Math.abs(y)), 0) / L.length;
  return {L, d, simple};
}

NoteGo deeper: why the data can’t choose a rotation

Assumptions: the common factor model with \(m \ge 2\) factors, loadings in a \(p \times m\) matrix \(\Lambda\), factor correlations \(\Phi\) (the identity for uncorrelated factors), and unique variances in a diagonal matrix \(\Psi\).

The implied correlations. Stacking the model for all \(p\) items gives \(x = \Lambda f + u\), and so \[\text{cor}(x) = \Lambda \Phi \Lambda^\top + \Psi.\]

Orthogonal rotation. Let \(T\) be any orthogonal \(m \times m\) matrix (\(T T^\top = I\)), and take new loadings \(\Lambda^* = \Lambda T\) with \(\Phi = I\). Then \[\Lambda^* \Lambda^{*\top} = \Lambda T T^\top \Lambda^\top = \Lambda \Lambda^\top,\] so every implied correlation is unchanged. The widget’s \(T\) is the \(2 \times 2\) rotation by the slider’s angle.

Oblique rotation. Let \(T\) be any invertible matrix, and take \(\Lambda^* = \Lambda (T^{\top})^{-1}\) and \(\Phi^* = T^\top \Phi T\). Then \(\Lambda^* \Phi^* \Lambda^{*\top} = \Lambda (T^{\top})^{-1} T^\top \Phi T\, T^{-1} \Lambda^\top = \Lambda \Phi \Lambda^\top\). (With the factor variances held at 1, \(T\) must keep the diagonal of \(\Phi^*\) at 1.)

What it means. The likelihood, the residuals and every fit statistic depend on the data only through the implied correlations, so they cannot prefer one \(T\) over another. A rotation criterion (varimax, oblimin, …) is an extra rule for choosing \(T\), and it is a choice about description. The same indeterminacy reappears when a confirmatory model has to fix loadings to be identified (Factor analysis II) and in multidimensional item response models (Dimensionality and multidimensional IRT). If you’ve done the Rasch lesson, it is a cousin of the scale’s missing origin: the data fix differences, not the frame they are described in.

Rotations come in two kinds (Browne, 2001, reviews both). Orthogonal rotations keep the factors uncorrelated; varimax (Kaiser, 1958) is the best known. Oblique rotations let them correlate: oblimin (in R’s default form, direct quartimin; Jennrich & Sampson, 1966), promax (Hendrickson & White, 1964) and others. In psychology and education, constructs tend to correlate with one another, so forcing factors to be uncorrelated is an assumption we seldom have reason to make. My default is oblique. If the factors turn out to be nearly uncorrelated, the two approaches will agree anyway, and we’ll see below how far from zero the BFI-2’s factor correlations sit.

Two practical notes

Likert items are not continuous. Pearson correlations between 5-point items understate the relationship between the underlying continuous tendencies, and they are distorted when items are skewed. The standard fix is to factor-analyze polychoric correlations (Olsson, 1979), which estimate the correlation between the continuous variables that the ordered categories are assumed to coarsen. We use them below. This idea, an ordered response as a coarsened continuous one, is also the bridge to item response theory; see Factor analysis II.

Factor analysis is not principal components analysis. PCA (Pearson, 1901; Hotelling, 1933) finds weighted sums of the items that capture as much of their total variance as possible; it has no unique parts and no model of measurement error. Factor analysis models only the shared variance. For summarizing data the two often look similar (Velicer & Jackson, 1990); for asking what constructs an instrument measures, factor analysis is the right tool (Fabrigar et al., 1999). Problem 6 compares the two on real data.

Parallel analysis says 9, the Kaiser rule 12, against a design of five domains and 15 facets. Is that unusual? Across the IRW it is the norm. The IRW’s dimensionality vignette ran parallel analysis on 810 tables and found exactly one factor in only 3% of them; for the older 25-item Big Five Inventory in psychtools_bfi, built for five traits, it suggests six. Nor is it an artifact of the polychoric correlations, which Garrido, Abad and Ponsoda (2013) recommend for parallel analysis with ordinal items: on Pearson correlations the answer is 9 again. The formal criteria find more structure than the design names, because each domain is itself made of related facets.

The same vignette gives a baseline for how multidimensional the BFI-2 is. The ratio of the first to the second eigenvalue, 1.7 here, is a rough index of how far a scale is from a single dimension (larger means closer to one). The vignette’s 57 personality tables have a median ratio of 3.26, and the 25-item BFI’s is 1.91. The vignette’s tables have at most 40 items, so the comparison is rough, but by the IRW’s standard the BFI-2 is clearly multidimensional, as it was built to be.

The five-domain structure is still the one the instrument is built to score, so that is what we fit. The choice is ours; the data inform it but do not make it.

Identical root-mean-square residuals: the orthogonal and oblique solutions fit exactly as well, because they are the same model described in two frames. Rotation is a choice about how to read the factors, never about how well they fit.

Problems

  1. One factor. Show that under a one-factor model with standardized items and factor, \(\text{cor}(x_i, x_j) = \lambda_i\lambda_j\) for \(i \ne j\). Then show that, for any four items, \(\text{cor}(x_1,x_3)\,\text{cor}(x_2,x_4) = \text{cor}(x_1,x_4)\,\text{cor}(x_2,x_3)\). (These “tetrad” constraints were Spearman’s test for a single factor; Spearman & Holzinger, 1924.)
  2. Reliability, revisited. In a one-factor model, what is the correlation between the sum of the items and the factor? Use your answer to explain why items with low loadings contribute little to reliability, and connect it to the proof that alpha is a lower bound in the reliability lesson. What does tau-equivalence mean in terms of loadings?
  3. Planning a pilot. You are piloting a new 15-item scale written for three factors, and you expect loadings of about 0.5 and factor correlations of about 0.3. Using the simulation, find the smallest sample (to the nearest 50) at which parallel analysis finds three factors in at least 9 of 10 seeds. How does your answer change if the loadings are 0.4? What does this suggest about relying on one rule in a small pilot?
  4. Formats. The BFI-2 data come from four randomly assigned response formats (itemcov_format: Likert L, Expanded E, and two item-specific versions, ISF and ISL). Fit the five-factor model separately in each format. Do the same items load on the same factors? Are the loadings noticeably stronger in any format? What would a difference tell you about the formats, and why does random assignment matter for that inference? (Zhang et al., 2025, is the place to compare notes; Factor analysis II gives the formal test of whether a structure holds across groups.)
  5. A weak item. Find the three BFI-2 items with the lowest communalities in the five-factor solution. Look them up in Soto and John (2017), where the item wording is published. Would you drop them? What might they be measuring instead?
  6. Challenge: PCA versus FA. Run principal() (PCA) and fa() with five components or factors on the BFI-2. Compare the loadings. Which are systematically larger, and why? Is there a number of items per domain at which the difference stops mattering?

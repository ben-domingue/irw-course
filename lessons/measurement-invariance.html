What this is for

In a Dutch community sample, women report a little more anxiety than men on the DASS-21 (0.16 SD, as we’ll see). Is that a difference in anxiety, or a difference in how the items work for women and men? The scores alone can’t say. Factor analysis II fitted a factor model to one sample. This lesson fits it to two groups at once and asks, one constraint at a time, whether the same model holds in both: measurement invariance. Where it holds, the groups’ factor means can be compared. Where an item breaks it, we find the item and decide what to do about it. We start with a scale where invariance holds, the DASS-21 across women and men, and then turn to one where a single item doesn’t: crying on the CES-D.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • fit a multigroup CFA in lavaan and say how the two groups are put on one scale;
  • climb the ladder (configural, metric, scalar) with scaled difference tests and changes in CFI, and say which comparison each step supports;
  • find the constraint that fails with score tests, and fit and report a partial invariance model;
  • say what one unequal intercept does to a comparison of sum scores and of factor means;
  • choose an estimator for a multigroup fit, and say what ordered categories change.

Core ideas

One model, two groups

Recall from Factor analysis II: a confirmatory model fixes most loadings at zero before seeing the data, and it is identified once each factor’s scale is set, by fixing one loading or the factor’s variance. Nested models are compared by a (scaled) difference in χ².

A multigroup CFA fits the model in several groups at once, with any parameter free in each group or held equal across them (Jöreskog, 1971). With intercepts, the model for item \(i\) in group \(g\) is \(x_i = \nu_{ig} + \lambda_{ig}\eta + \varepsilon_i\). Measurement invariance means that responses depend on the factor and not on the group (Meredith, 1993). It is tested in steps, each adding constraints to the one before (Vandenberg & Lance, 2000; Millsap, 2011; Putnick & Bornstein, 2016):

  • configural: the same items load on the same factors in every group, every parameter free;
  • metric: the loadings \(\lambda_i\) are equal, so a unit of the factor means the same in each group;
  • scalar: the intercepts \(\nu_i\) (for ordered items, the thresholds) are equal too, so respondents at the same factor level have the same expected response.

Meredith adds a fourth step, strict invariance, with equal unique variances as well.

Why these comparisons at these steps? A factor has no unit and no origin of its own. In the configural model each group’s factor is scaled separately, so nothing links them. Once the loadings are equal, the items carry one unit across groups, and group B’s factor variance, and its correlations and regressions, can be estimated relative to group A’s. Once the intercepts are equal too, the items carry one origin, and group B’s factor mean can be estimated relative to A’s; lavaan frees it at that step. Comparing correlations or regressions needs metric invariance; comparing means needs scalar.

In item response language, an unequal loading is non-uniform differential item functioning and an unequal intercept is uniform DIF; Differential item functioning takes up the same question item by item, matching on an observed score instead of a factor.

What an unequal intercept does

Try it: one item that works differently

Four items measure one factor in groups A and B (loadings 0.7, intercepts 0, factor SD 1). Item 4 can behave differently in group B.

With both item-4 controls at 0 the lines coincide, and the sum-score gap (0.45 SD) is the latent 0.5 SD shrunk by unreliability. Set the true gap to 0 and item 4’s intercept to 0.3: the sum scores differ by 0.10 SD between groups that don’t differ at all. A loading difference makes the lines cross, so the item favours one group at low factor levels and the other at high ones.

What does the factor model make of the same item? If we impose scalar invariance anyway, the model has to explain item 4’s higher mean in group B with the one thing it has left free, group B’s factor mean. The shift is spread over every item, and the factor mean absorbs part of it. The remedy is partial invariance (Byrne, Shavelson & Muthén, 1989): free the offending item’s intercept and keep the others equal, so that the comparison of means rests on the items that do work alike.

At the start, the groups don’t differ, yet the scalar model puts group B 0.107 SD above A. With equal loadings the error is the shift divided by \(k\lambda\) (problem 1), here \(0.3/(4 \times 0.7)\). The partial model recovers the truth. With 300 respondents per group, the scalar step fails 97% of the time; halve the shift and the error halves while the chance of catching it falls to 44%. Raise \(k\) to 12 and the error falls to 0.036: more invariant items dilute one shifted one. Move the true mean: the error stays the same size, whatever the groups’ real difference.

Testing the steps

Each step is nested in the one before, so a χ² difference tests it, scaled when the estimator is robust (Satorra & Bentler, 2001). As with any χ² test, a large enough sample rejects a step for differences too small to matter. So changes in fit indices are read alongside: Cheung and Rensvold (2002) proposed treating a drop in CFI of more than 0.01 as a failed step, and Chen (2007) paired it with changes in RMSEA and SRMR; Putnick and Bornstein (2016) survey how these criteria are used and questioned.

When a step fails, which constraint is responsible? A score test (lavTestScore() in lavaan) says, for each equality constraint, how much the χ² would fall if that constraint alone were released. The largest is the first suspect. But a scalar model with many items holds dozens of constraints, and a few small \(p\)-values turn up by chance.

Try it: how many flags by chance?

Code 
md`If every one of ${fl_m} constraints were true and the tests were independent, at least one would fall below ${fl_a} with probability **${fl[fl_m - 1].p.toFixed(2)}**. A Bonferroni threshold would be ${fl_a} / ${fl_m} = **${(fl_a / fl_m).toPrecision(2)}**.`

At 39 constraints and α = 0.01, a flag turns up by chance about a third of the time (0.32); at α = 0.05, 86% of the time. Score tests on one model aren’t independent, so these are rough guides, but they set the baseline for reading the largest test.

Choosing an estimator for multigroup fits

Recall from Factor analysis II: choose the estimator from the data. ML for continuous, roughly normal items; MLR for skewed items or five or more categories used fairly evenly; WLSMV, on thresholds and polychorics, for fewer than five categories or responses piled at one end; FIML, or the graded model, when responses are missing.

In a multigroup fit the guide applies group by group, and three things change. With ordered items each group gets its own thresholds, so every category of every item needs responses in each group; a category that is nearly empty in one group is a problem even when it isn’t in the pooled data (Rutkowski, Svetina & Liaw, 2019, who also show that collapsing categories changes fit). Which parameters must be fixed at each step to keep ordinal models identified and nested is subtler than with continuous items (Wu & Estabrook, 2016). And the difference tests behave differently across estimators: in simulations by Sass, Schmitt and Marsh (2014), their Type I error rates varied across ML, MLR and WLSMV, and changes in fit indices under WLSMV needed caution in misspecified models. With missing ordinal responses, WLSMV with pairwise deletion was the one method in the simulations of Chen, Wu, Garnier-Villarreal, Kite and Jia (2020) whose difference tests did not reasonably control their Type I error rate; the full-information ordinal models recovered loadings best.

So a reasonable plan with sparse categories is MLR for the ladder, items as numbers, with an ordinal fit as the check, on collapsed categories if need be.

Simulate

The code simulates 500 respondents in each of two groups answering six items that measure one factor, with group B’s factor mean 0.3 SD higher and item 6’s intercept 0.4 higher. It climbs the ladder with lavaan, runs the score tests on the scalar model, frees the item with the largest one, and compares group B’s estimated factor mean with the truth. The first run installs lavaan, which takes a little while; after that, each run takes a few seconds.

With the seed as written, configural to metric passes (\(p = 0.31\)) and the scalar step fails (Δχ² = 42.3 on 5 df); item 6’s intercept has by far the largest score test (40.5); and group B’s mean comes out at 0.377 under full scalar invariance and 0.319 with item 6 free (truth 0.3). Set shift <- 0 and the scalar step should pass; set n <- 150 and see how often the test still finds item 6 across a few seeds.

# Which equality constraint strains most? One score test per constraint.
score_tests <- function(fit) {
  st <- lavTestScore(fit)$uni
  pt <- parTable(fit)
  st$constraint <- sapply(st$lhs, function(l) with(pt[pt$plabel == l, ], paste(lhs, op, rhs)))
  st[order(-st$X2.scaled), c("constraint", "X2.scaled", "df", "p.value.scaled")]
}

# Group 2's factor means under scalar invariance, in units of group 1's factor SD.
latent_means <- function(fit, factors) {
  pe <- parameterEstimates(fit)
  mu2 <- pe[pe$op == "~1" & pe$lhs %in% factors & pe$group == 2, c("lhs", "est", "se")]
  sd1 <- sqrt(diag(lavInspect(fit, "est")[[1]]$psi))
  data.frame(factor = mu2$lhs, mean_diff = round(mu2$est, 3), se = round(mu2$se, 3),
             in_group1_SDs = round(mu2$est / sd1[mu2$lhs], 2))
}

So what should a report say? One view drops the crying item, so that the scale is invariant as it stands. Another keeps it and frees its parameters, so that the comparison rests on the other 19 items while the item still counts toward each respondent’s score. A third ignores the failure because it is one item in twenty. The first changes the instrument, and its scores no longer match published norms; the third lets one item push the comparison, by 0.03 SD here; the second needs enough invariant items to carry the comparison. When enough items hold, freeing the item and reporting the comparison both ways, with the item and the reason named, may be sufficient for some purposes: a reader can see how much rests on the one item. Here the two give 0.21 and 0.18 SD, and the conclusion doesn’t change.

Problems

  1. The size of the error. Four items with equal loadings \(\lambda\) and unique variances \(\psi\) measure one factor in two groups, with item 4’s intercept higher by \(\delta\) in group B. (a) Show that the gap between the groups’ expected sum scores is \(4\lambda\kappa + \delta\), and express it in SDs of group A’s sum score, as the first widget does. (b) With \(k\) items, show that the scalar model’s population estimate of group B’s mean is \(\kappa + \delta/(k\lambda)\). (Hint: with the covariances fitted exactly, the scalar model’s mean part is a generalized least-squares regression of the item mean differences on \(\lambda\).)
  2. A different grouping. Split the CES-D respondents at the median of cov_age and climb the ladder with the same two-factor model. Does the crying item stand out again? Which constraints do, and what would you do about them?
  3. Significant but small. A scale is compared across two groups of 10,000 respondents each. The scalar step gives a scaled Δχ² of 180 on 20 df (\(p < .001\)) and a drop in CFI of 0.003. Is the scale invariant enough to compare means? What would you look at before deciding, and what would you report?
  4. Design. You want to compare DASS-21 scores across the education levels in cov_education. Which level of invariance do you need to compare means, and which to compare the depression–anxiety correlation? Check the group sizes first: which estimator would you use, and what would you do if one item’s intercept failed in one group?
  5. Nested or not? Using the code’s ordinal DASS fits, list what each of the three models fixes and frees (thresholds, scale factors, factor means and variances). Why is the default metric-to-scalar difference not a test of equal thresholds? Build a nested sequence as Wu and Estabrook (2016) propose (the semTools function measEq.syntax() writes the syntax), test it, and compare the verdict with the MLR ladder.
  6. Challenge (open). With thirty countries, Asparouhov and Muthén (2014) note, the scalar model tends to fit poorly and to throw up many large modification indices. They propose alignment, which estimates the countries’ factor means without requiring exact invariance. What does alignment assume in place of invariance, and how would you decide whether the means it gives are trustworthy enough to rank the countries? I don’t know of a settled answer.

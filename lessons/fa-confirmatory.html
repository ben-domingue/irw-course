What this is for

The Grit-O scale was written around two facets of grit, perseverance of effort and consistency of interest. Does it measure one thing or two? Factor analysis I let the data propose a structure. This lesson states a structure first and asks whether the data agree: confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). A written-down structure also answers four more questions. How well does it fit? How reliable is a score built on it (omega)? What is it, seen from item response theory? And does it hold in two groups, so that their means can be compared (measurement invariance)? Along the way we settle whether the DASS-21 yields one score or three.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • write a confirmatory factor model in lavaan syntax, say how it is identified, and count its degrees of freedom;
  • read χ², CFI, TLI, RMSEA and SRMR, say what each responds to, and compare nested models;
  • compute omega and omega hierarchical, and say when omega equals alpha;
  • convert an ordinal factor loading and threshold into a slope and a difficulty, and check the conversion against mirt;
  • test configural, metric and scalar invariance across two groups, and say which one a comparison of means needs.

Core ideas

Stating the structure first

A confirmatory model is the common factor model with most loadings fixed at zero before the data are seen. For grit, perseverance items load on one factor and consistency items on the other, with each item’s other loading fixed at 0. Jöreskog (1969) showed how to fit such a model by maximum likelihood and test it. We use the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012), whose syntax reads almost like a path diagram. =~ means “is measured by”, and an item left off a line has its loading on that factor fixed at zero:

two <- "per =~ g1 + g4 + g6 + g9 + g10 + g12
        con =~ g2 + g3 + g5 + g7 + g8 + g11"
fit <- cfa(two, data = grit)

The factors may correlate (lavaan’s default), and each item keeps a unique variance.

Recall from Factor analysis I: with two or more factors, any rotation reproduces the correlations exactly as well as any other. The data fix the correlations, not the frame of the factors.

The zeros pin the frame, since rotating would move them. One indeterminacy is left: a factor has no natural unit. The model is identified once each factor’s scale is set, by fixing one loading per factor to 1 (lavaan’s default) or each factor’s variance to 1 (std.lv = TRUE). The two choices fit identically and report loadings in different units, as the grit output shows.

What can the data test? They supply \(p(p+1)/2\) variances and covariances. The model spends one parameter on each free loading, unique variance and factor correlation. The degrees of freedom are the difference, and each one is a constraint the data could violate.

Fit indices, and what they can’t tell you

Every fit index compares the covariance matrix the model implies with the one the data supply. The likelihood-ratio χ² tests exact fit: it is \(N - 1\) times the discrepancy, so a large sample rejects any model that is slightly wrong, and every model is. The other indices ask how wrong. RMSEA is the misfit per degree of freedom (Steiger, 1990). CFI (Bentler, 1990) and TLI (Tucker & Lewis, 1973) compare the model’s misfit with that of a baseline in which the items are uncorrelated. SRMR is the root mean square residual correlation.

Hu and Bentler (1999) found, in simulations of maximum-likelihood estimation, that cutoffs near 0.95 for CFI and TLI, 0.06 for RMSEA and 0.08 for SRMR were needed to call a fit good. Marsh, Hau and Wen (2004) argue against reading them as fixed rules, and with ordinal estimators the same indices tell a different story (Xia & Yang, 2019). They describe how far off a model is; the residuals show where.

Omega: reliability from the model

Recall from Classical test theory and reliability: alpha is a lower bound on reliability, with equality under essential tau-equivalence. Factor analysis I read tau-equivalence as a one-factor model with equal loadings.

A factor model makes the bound unnecessary. Under a one-factor model with loadings \(\lambda_i\) and unique variances \(\psi_i\), the sum score has variance \((\sum_i \lambda_i)^2 + \sum_i \psi_i\), of which \((\sum_i\lambda_i)^2\) comes from the factor. Their ratio is omega (McDonald, 1999): \[\omega = \frac{(\sum_i \lambda_i)^2}{(\sum_i \lambda_i)^2 + \sum_i \psi_i}.\] When the loadings are equal, omega equals alpha (problem 1). When they aren’t, alpha is smaller, usually by a little (Revelle & Zinbarg, 2009).

Omega raises a sharper question when a scale has subscales. In a bifactor model (Holzinger & Swineford, 1937; Reise, 2012), every item loads on a general factor and on one specific factor for its subscale, with all factors uncorrelated. Omega total counts the general and specific factors as signal. Omega hierarchical, \(\omega_h\), counts only the general factor: it is the share of the total score’s variance that one common construct accounts for (Rodriguez, Reise & Haviland, 2016).

Item response theory as factor analysis

Recall from From the 1PL to the 4PL: the 2PL gives each item a slope \(a\) and a difficulty \(b\); mirt writes \(a\theta + d\), so \(b = -d/a\).

Factor analysis I treated an ordered response as a coarsened continuous one and factored polychoric correlations. Write that down for one item: a latent response \(y^* = \lambda\eta + \varepsilon\), with \(\text{var}(y^*) = 1\), and \(x = 1\) when \(y^*\) passes a threshold \(\tau\). Then \[\Pr(x = 1 \mid \eta) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\lambda\eta - \tau}{\sqrt{1 - \lambda^2}}\right) = \Phi\big(a(\eta - b)\big), \quad a = \frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{1-\lambda^2}},\; b = \frac{\tau}{\lambda}.\] That is the normal-ogive 2PL. The factor model for dichotomous items is an item response model (Takane & de Leeuw, 1987), and with more categories each threshold becomes a category boundary of the graded response model (Samejima, 1969; Kamata & Bauer, 2008). For the logistic metric of mirt (Chalmers, 2012) and this course, multiply the slope by about 1.7 (Camilli, 1994, on where the constant comes from); this is the only place in the course it appears.

NoteGo deeper: ordinal factor analysis is the graded response model

Assumptions: an item with ordered categories \(0, \dots, K\); a latent response \(y^* = \lambda^\top \eta + \varepsilon\) with \(\eta\) multivariate normal (variances 1), \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, 1 - h^2)\) independent of \(\eta\) and of the other items’ errors, where \(h^2 = \text{var}(\lambda^\top\eta)\) is the communality, so \(\text{var}(y^*) = 1\). The response is \(x = k\) when \(\tau_k < y^* \le \tau_{k+1}\), with \(\tau_0 = -\infty\) and \(\tau_{K+1} = \infty\).

Boundary curves. For \(k = 1, \dots, K\), \[\Pr(x \ge k \mid \eta) = \Pr(\varepsilon > \tau_k - \lambda^\top\eta) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\lambda^\top\eta - \tau_k}{\sqrt{1 - h^2}}\right).\] With one factor this is \(\Phi\big(a(\eta - b_k)\big)\) with \(a = \lambda/\sqrt{1-\lambda^2}\) and \(b_k = \tau_k/\lambda\): one slope per item and one boundary per category, which is Samejima’s (1969) normal-ogive graded response model. Category probabilities are differences of adjacent boundary curves. With several factors, the slope on factor \(m\) is \(a_m = \lambda_m/\sqrt{1 - h^2}\).

Local independence. The errors are independent across items, so given \(\eta\) the responses are independent. Item response theory’s local independence is the factor model’s uncorrelated unique parts.

Logistic metric. \(\sup_z |\Phi(z) - \text{logit}^{-1}(1.702z)| < 0.01\) (the widget’s gap), so a logistic slope is about \(1.702a\); in mirt’s form, \(d_k = -1.702\,a\,b_k\).

Why the numbers differ in practice. The model is the same; the estimators are not. lavaan’s WLSMV estimates thresholds and polychoric correlations, which use the data one or two items at a time (Muthén, 1984; Flora & Curran, 2004). mirt maximizes the likelihood of whole response patterns. When the model holds they converge on the same values as the sample grows; in a finite sample they differ by sampling error, by the logistic approximation, and wherever the model misfits, since the two estimators weigh the misfit differently.

When can Likert items be treated as numbers? In simulations by Rhemtulla, Brosseau-Liard and Savalei (2012), robust maximum likelihood kept up with the categorical estimator given five or more roughly symmetric categories, not fewer. Grit has five balanced categories; the DASS-21 has four, piled at zero.

Does the model hold in both groups?

Do women and men with the same level of anxiety answer an anxiety item the same way? Measurement invariance (Meredith, 1993) asks this of a factor model fitted to two or more groups at once, in steps that add constraints (Vandenberg & Lance, 2000; Millsap, 2011; Putnick & Bornstein, 2016):

  • configural: the same items load on the same factors in every group;
  • metric: the loadings are equal, so a unit of the factor means the same thing in each group;
  • scalar: the intercepts (for ordinal items, the thresholds) are equal too, so the same factor level predicts the same response.

Comparing factor correlations across groups needs metric invariance; comparing means needs scalar. When one item fails, partial invariance frees its parameters and keeps the rest equal. In item response language, an unequal loading is non-uniform differential item functioning (DIF) and an unequal intercept is uniform DIF, which Differential item functioning takes up item by item. With ordinal items, which parameters are identified at each step is subtler than with continuous ones (Wu & Estabrook, 2016).

The DASS-21: three scales or one?

Our contrast table is neurodegenerative_huizinga_2019_dass: the Dutch DASS-21, answered by a community sample of adults recruited by Huizinga et al. (2020) for a study of visual complaints. Its job: a structure whose designed scales may not be distinct, and then invariance. The DASS-21 scores depression, anxiety and stress with seven items each (Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995; Henry & Crawford, 2005). Every item is worded toward more distress, and the scales follow the DASS scoring template.

Should a clinic report three DASS scores or one? The subscales add information only through their specific factors, which are small here, and the DASS authors describe the total as a sensible composite (DASS FAQ, no. 26). For a community sample like this one, I would report the total, and the depression and anxiety scores only alongside it. A clinical sample could give a different answer.

Invariance: women and men

The table’s cov_sex codes are unlabelled in the processing script. The paper reports 785 women among its 1,461 participants (its Table 1), and code 2 holds exactly 785 of the table’s 1,461 respondents, so we read 2 as women: a match of counts, not a codebook. Problems

  1. Omega and alpha. Show that under a one-factor model with equal loadings \(\lambda\) and equal unique variances, omega equals alpha. Then show that with unequal loadings (and uncorrelated errors) alpha is smaller.
  2. The probit link. Starting from \(y^* = \lambda\eta + \varepsilon\) with \(\text{var}(y^*) = 1\), derive the normal-ogive slope and difficulty of a dichotomized item. Use your result on grit: which facet’s items discriminate better, and does mirt agree?
  3. A patch or a finding? (a) Run modindices() on the two-factor grit model. Among the largest suggestions is a correlated error between items 3 and 11. Add it. How much does fit improve? Given the codebook’s stems (“My interests…”, “I become…”), is it a finding about the construct or a patch? Can any model of these data separate consistency of interest from wording direction (From construct map to items)? (b) Dichotomize the keyed items (4 or 5 against the rest), fit the one- and two-factor models as binary CFAs, and compare them by the IMV on held-out respondents with five-fold cross-validation, as Zhang et al. (2026) do. (Model fit and out-of-sample prediction introduces the IMV.) Does prediction tell the same story as the fit indices, and for every item?
  4. Where invariance breaks. Fit a one-factor model to the 20-item CES-D (Radloff, 1977) in alexandrowicz_2018_cesd (Alexandrowicz, Jahn & Wancata, 2018) across cov_sex, and climb the ladder. Where does it fail? Free the constraint with the largest score test and compare the latent means with and without it. (The paper reports 264 women of 518; the table has 263 coded 2 of 514, so 2 is probably women, but nothing documents it.)
  5. Design. You want to compare DASS-21 scores across the education levels in cov_education. Which level of invariance do you need to compare means, and which to compare the depression–anxiety correlation? What would you do if one item’s intercept failed in one group? Check the group sizes first.
  6. Challenge (open). Credé, Tynan and Harms (2017) argue from a meta-analysis that grit overlaps heavily with conscientiousness. What confirmatory model would test that, and what data would you need? I don’t know of a settled answer.

What this is for

A respondent scores 12 on the PHQ-9, a nine-item depression screener. What does the 12 mean? On its own, not much. It means something once we say whom it is compared with, how far it might move, and which side of a decision it falls on. Classical test theory and reliability gave every score one standard error; Information, precision, and short forms let the error change along the scale. This lesson puts both to work on the numbers respondents are given. The example is the PHQ-9 in a UK panel surveyed in the first week of the 2020 lockdown, with a German sample for contrast.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • turn a raw score into a percentile rank, z score, linear T and normalized T, and say what each assumes;
  • put an error band around a score, from one SEM or a conditional SEM, and say when the two differ;
  • compute the reliability of a difference score and a reliable change threshold;
  • treat a cut score as a decision, do an Angoff calculation, and say why classification near a cut is uncertain;
  • say what a score report should carry, and why group statistics use plausible values.

Core ideas

A score needs a reference

A raw score becomes interpretable in one of two ways (Glaser, 1963). A norm-referenced reading compares it with other people’s scores; a criterion-referenced reading compares it with a standard fixed in advance, such as the screener’s cut of 10. The Standards ask that users be told what a reported score means and where its meaning runs out (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, open access, Standard 5.1). Cut scores come later; first, norms.

The standard norm-referenced scores are (Angoff, 1971; Kolen & Brennan, 2014, ch. 9):

  • the percentile rank: the percentage of the reference group scoring below, plus half of those scoring the same;
  • the z score, \((x - \bar x)/s\), and the linear T score, \(50 + 10z\);
  • the normalized T score, \(50 + 10\,\Phi^{-1}(\text{percentile rank}/100)\), which puts the percentile rank on a normal scale. PROMIS reports its measures as T scores centred on a reference population (Cella et al., 2010).

Recall from What is measurement?: if a score is only ordinal, any order-preserving rescaling of it is as legitimate as the original, and only conclusions that survive every such rescaling are safe.

A percentile rank uses nothing but order, so it survives every rescaling and needs no claim about equal units. A linear T keeps the raw distribution’s shape and spacing; a normalized T imposes a normal shape. On a skewed distribution, such as PHQ-9 scores in the general population, the two part in the tail.

Each is a comparison with one group at one time; the Standards ask that norms describe that group clearly and be renewed often enough to stay accurate (Standards 5.8 and 5.9).

Error bands: one SEM, or a conditional one

Recall from Classical test theory and reliability: the standard error of measurement, \(\sigma_E = \sigma_X\sqrt{1 - \rho_{XX'}}\), is the typical distance between a respondent’s observed and true scores, and CTT gives the same one to everyone, whatever their score.

The simplest band is \(x \pm 1.96\,\sigma_E\), the true scores consistent with what we observed (Lord & Novick, 1968). With the PHQ-9’s SD of 6.22 and alpha of 0.92 in the UK sample below, the SEM is 1.74 and the band ±3.4 points around every score: a 12 has a band from 8.6 to 15.4, which straddles the cut.

Recall from Information, precision, and short forms: test information \(I(\theta)\) is the sum of item information, and the conditional SEM, \(1/\sqrt{I(\theta)}\), is small where many informative items sit and grows where they run out. A screening scale spends its precision where it makes its decisions.

The conditional SEM gives bands that change width along the scale, and the Standards ask for the standard error near each cut score (Standard 2.14; Kolen, Zeng & Hanson, 1996, carry conditional SEMs to a reported scale). The PHQ-9’s items have four categories, so the model below is the graded response model (Samejima, 1969), with one slope per item and a boundary between adjacent categories (Models for polytomous responses develops it). All we need here is that its information adds over items, and that it says how much the sum score varies across replications at a given \(\theta\).

If you’ve done Factor analysis II: omega can stand in for alpha in \(\sigma_X\sqrt{1 - \rho}\). It changes the size of the one SEM, not the fact that there is one.

Difference scores and reliable change

When the question is whether a respondent changed, the score of interest is a difference, \(D = X_2 - X_1\). For two occasions with equal variances, reliabilities \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho_2\) and correlation \(r_{12}\), its reliability is \[\rho_D = \frac{(\rho_1 + \rho_2)/2 - r_{12}}{1 - r_{12}}\] (Lord, 1956). The higher the occasions correlate, the less of the difference is true change. Cronbach and Furby (1970) asked whether change should be measured this way at all; Rogosa and Willett (1983) showed that when respondents differ appreciably in how much their true scores change, the difference can be quite reliable. Which applies depends on the data.

For one respondent, does the change exceed what error alone would produce? The reliable change index of Jacobson and Truax (1991) divides the change by the standard error of a difference, \(\sqrt 2\,\sigma_E\), so a change counts as reliable at the 5% level when \(|x_2 - x_1| \ge 1.96\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_E\). The Standards ask for precision data for such differences (Standard 2.4).

Cut scores are decisions

A cut score turns a score into a category. Where does the number come from? There are two routes, and both end in a choice.

The empirical route sets the cut against an outside criterion. The PHQ-9’s 10 was chosen against diagnostic interviews: in its first validation a score of 10 or more had a sensitivity and a specificity of 88% for major depression (Kroenke, Spitzer & Williams, 2001), and an individual-participant meta-analysis found that 10 maximized the two combined (Levis, Benedetti & Thombs, 2019). An earlier meta-analysis, though, found no substantial differences in pooled sensitivity and specificity across cuts from 8 to 11 (Manea, Gilbody & McMillan, 2012). The data narrow the range; choosing within it trades missed cases against false alarms. Accuracy against the criterion belongs to Validity evidence.

The judgmental route asks experts. In the Angoff method (Angoff, 1971; Cizek & Bunch, 2007, ch. 6) each judge pictures a borderline respondent, just good enough to pass, and estimates for every item the probability that this respondent gets it right. A judge’s cut is the sum of their probabilities; the panel’s is the mean over judges, with a standard error from their spread. The bookmark method orders items by difficulty and asks where the borderline respondent’s mastery stops (Cizek & Bunch, 2007, ch. 10).

Either way, the Standards ask that a cut’s rationale be documented and, where feasible, informed by data on the criterion (Standards 5.21 and 5.23). And a respondent whose true score sits at the cut would screen positive on about half of hypothetical replications; the Go deeper callout turns that into a number for a test.

NoteGo deeper: classification consistency at a cut

Assumptions: an item response model holds with known item parameters; responses are locally independent given \(\theta\); two administrations are independent given \(\theta\) (parallel forms, or the same form with no memory of the first); respondents are classified by their sum score \(X\) against a cut \(C\).

Given \(\theta\). Local independence lets us compute the sum score’s distribution at each \(\theta\) exactly, by adding items one at a time (Lord & Wingersky, 1984). Start with \(f_0(0) = 1\); after item \(i\), with category probabilities \(P_{ik}(\theta)\) for scores \(k = 0, 1, \dots\), \[f_i(s) = \sum_k f_{i-1}(s - k)\,P_{ik}(\theta).\] Then \(p(\theta) = \Pr(X \ge C \mid \theta) = \sum_{s \ge C} f_I(s)\), and two independent administrations agree with probability \[\kappa(\theta) = p(\theta)^2 + \big(1 - p(\theta)\big)^2,\] which is near 1 far from the cut and falls to 1/2 where \(p(\theta) = 1/2\).

Over respondents. The test’s classification consistency is the average of \(\kappa(\theta)\) over the ability distribution, \(\int \kappa(\theta)\,g(\theta)\,d\theta\), with \(g\) the fitted distribution, which needs only one administration. Lee (2010) sets out this approach for tests that mix dichotomous and polytomous items. The average is high when most respondents sit far from the cut, whatever happens near it, so a consistency above 0.9 can coexist with near coin-flip classification for the respondents closest to the cut. The Simulate section checks both against two simulated administrations.

Without an item response model. Livingston and Lewis (1995) build the same quantity from classical ingredients: the reliability gives an effective test length, a four-parameter beta distribution stands in for the true scores, and a binomial for the error around them.

What goes in a score report

Reviews of how score reports are designed and read suggest giving the score with its band, the reference group and its date, the cut and what crossing it means, and what the score does not show (Goodman & Hambleton, 2004; Zenisky & Hambleton, 2012; Standard 6.10). A PHQ-9 report would add that the manual treats 10 or more as a flag for a possible clinically significant condition, not a diagnosis (PHQ instruction manual, n.d., p. 2).

If you’ve done Estimating abilities: EAP estimates shrink toward the mean, so averaging them understates differences between groups. That lesson’s simulation makes the point with numbers.

Reports about groups differ: the best estimate for each respondent is not the best input for describing the group. Estimates that borrow from a prior, such as the posterior mean (EAP; Estimating abilities), are pulled toward the centre, most where the test is imprecise, so their spread is smaller than the population’s. Plausible values are draws from each respondent’s posterior instead of its mean; a statistic computed on each set of draws and combined recovers the population’s spread and carries measurement error into its standard error (Mislevy, Beaton, Kaplan & Sheehan, 1992; Wu, 2005). They are for group statistics only.

Simulate

5,000 respondents take a PHQ-like graded test twice, with a cut at 10. How often does a respondent land on the same side both times? Compare the model’s prediction (the Go deeper callout) and CTT’s, which treats the two scores as normal with correlation alpha. Base R; seconds.

With the seed as written, 92.7% are classified the same way twice; the model predicts 93.1% and CTT’s normal approximation 90.4%, because the scores pile up at the floor, where a normal curve puts too few. Among the 10.4% whose chance of screening positive is between 0.2 and 0.8, only 55.6% are classified the same way twice. Try cut <- 5 and cut <- 20.

With real data

The main example is c19prc_uk_mcbride_2021_phq9: the PHQ-9 in the COVID-19 Psychological Research Consortium’s UK panel (Bentall et al., 2022). Wave 1 is an online sample of 2,025 adults with quotas on age, sex and household income, surveyed from 23 to 28 March 2020, the first week of lockdown; 1,406 were followed up a month later (McBride et al., 2021). Its job is to supply real norms, a real cut and two waves. The PHQ-9 asks how often in the last two weeks the respondent was bothered by each of nine symptoms, beginning with “Little interest or pleasure in doing things” (PHQ instruction manual, n.d., p. 5; Kroenke, Spitzer & Williams, 2001). Items are scored 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), none reversed. The code needs mirt (Chalmers, 2012); download it.

# Percentile rank: the percentage scoring below, plus half of those scoring exactly
# the same (the midpoint convention). It uses only the order of the scores.
pr <- function(score, ref) 100 * (mean(ref < score) + mean(ref == score) / 2)
band <- cut(age, c(17, 34, 54, Inf), labels = c("18-34", "35-54", "55+"))

The contrast is coroiu_2018_phq9, the same items in German, from adults representative by age and sex, interviewed at home in 2012 (Coroiu et al., 2018). Its job is to show how much a norm depends on its reference group.

What the comparison shows is how much a percentile rank depends on its reference group: the same 10 is the 79th percentile in one and the 96th in the other.

# The cut on the theta scale: where the expected PHQ-9 score (the test characteristic
# curve) reaches 10.
tcc <- function(t) expected.test(grm, matrix(t))
theta_cut <- uniroot(function(t) tcc(t) - 10, c(-4, 4))$root
csem <- function(t) 1 / sqrt(testinfo(grm, matrix(t)))
round(c(theta_cut = theta_cut, csem_at_cut = csem(theta_cut),
        csem_at_0 = csem(0), csem_at_minus_1 = csem(-1), csem_at_minus_2 = csem(-2)), 2)
      theta_cut     csem_at_cut       csem_at_0 csem_at_minus_1 csem_at_minus_2 
           0.86            0.21            0.27            0.77            2.80

The slopes run from 2.21 (Dep_3) to 3.61 (Dep_2), all above the 1 to 2.2 where most financial-literacy slopes fell in Information. The cut sits at \(\theta = 0.86\), where the conditional SEM is 0.21; it is 0.27 at \(\theta = 0\), 0.77 at \(-1\) and 2.80 at \(-2\). It is precise where the decision is made and says little about how far below it the 28.5% who scored 0 sit.

Code 
# The same precision on the raw-score scale: the SD of the PHQ-9 sum for respondents
# at a given theta, from the model's category probabilities.
raw_csem <- function(t) {
  v <- sapply(1:9, function(i) {
    p <- probtrace(extract.item(grm, i), matrix(t))
    sum((0:3)^2 * p) - sum((0:3) * p)^2
  })
  sqrt(sum(v))
}
at <- sapply(c(1, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25), function(s) uniroot(function(t) tcc(t) - s, c(-6, 6))$root)
# The two scales are linked by the slope of the test characteristic curve: an error of
# SE points in the score is an error of about SE / slope on theta. Where the curve is
# flat (near the floor and the ceiling), a small band in points is a wide band in theta.
slope <- sapply(at, function(t) (tcc(t + 0.001) - tcc(t - 0.001)) / 0.002)
round(rbind(raw = c(1, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25), theta = at,
            model_sem_raw = sapply(at, raw_csem), ctt_sem_raw = sem,
            tcc_slope = slope, sem_raw_over_slope = sapply(at, raw_csem) / slope,
            csem_theta = sapply(at, csem)), 2)

Divided by the expected-score curve’s slope (2.36 points per logit at a score of 1, 11.04 at 10), they become 0.44 and 0.21 on \(\theta\), close to the conditional SEMs of 0.41 and 0.21: narrow in points at the extremes, wide in \(\theta\).

# Bands on the theta scale: each respondent's estimate (EAP, mirt's default) plus or
# minus 1.96 conditional SEMs. How many positives have a band that reaches the cut?
th <- fscores(grm, verbose = FALSE)[, 1]

band_reaches_cut 
           0.562

With conditional-SEM bands on \(\theta\), 56.2% of the positives reach the cut, against 42.0% with CTT’s. Which should a report use? CTT’s band assumes the same error at every score; the model’s assumes its item curves and one \(\theta\) describe the data. At the cut the model’s SEM is about a third larger (2.36 points against 1.74). When a score will be read against a cut, I report the band from the conditional SEM at the cut, and give the one SEM only as a summary of the scale as a whole.

Change between waves

Code 
w2 <- long2wide(uk[uk$wave == 2, ])
both <- intersect(rownames(w1), rownames(w2))
x1 <- rowSums(w1[both, ]); x2 <- rowSums(w2[both, ])
r12 <- cor(x1, x2); a1 <- alpha(w1[both, ]); a2 <- alpha(w2[both, ])
# Reliability of the difference x2 - x1 (true-score variance of the difference over
# its observed variance)
rel_diff <- (a1 * var(x1) + a2 * var(x2) - 2 * cov(x1, x2)) / var(x2 - x1)
rci <- 1.96 * sqrt(2) * sem              # reliable change: |x2 - x1| at least this
dx <- x2 - x1
crossed <- (x1 >= 10) != (x2 >= 10)
round(c(n = length(both), r = r12, alpha_1 = a1, alpha_2 = a2, rel_diff = rel_diff,
        rci_threshold = rci, reliable_change = mean(abs(dx) >= rci),
        crossed_cut = mean(crossed), crossed_by_less = mean(crossed & abs(dx) < rci)), 3)
              n               r         alpha_1         alpha_2        rel_diff 
       1406.000           0.703           0.917           0.919           0.723 
  rci_threshold reliable_change     crossed_cut crossed_by_less

For the 1,406 seen twice, the waves correlate 0.70 and the change score has reliability 0.72. 19.6% changed by 4.8 points or more, the reliable change threshold. 13.9% crossed the cut, and 3.8% crossed it by less than 4.8 points: about a quarter of the crossings are within what error alone could produce. All this assumes the PHQ-9 measures the same thing at both waves, which Invariance and experience asks how to check.

Problems

  1. Derivation. Show that for two occasions with equal variances, reliabilities \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho_2\) and correlation \(r_{12}\), the difference score has reliability \(\big((\rho_1 + \rho_2)/2 - r_{12}\big)/(1 - r_{12})\) (Lord, 1956). When is it zero? Can it be negative, and what would that say about the classical assumptions?
  2. Which SEM for change? Recompute the reliable change threshold with the wave-2 alpha, and with the test–retest correlation (0.70) in place of alpha. How many respondents change reliably under each? Which would you use, and why?
  3. Old norms. A clinic in 2026 reads patients’ PHQ-9 scores against the UK spring-2020 percentiles. What would you tell them? Use Standard 5.9 and what the German comparison showed about reference groups.
  4. A report for one respondent. Design a one-page PHQ-9 report for a respondent who scored 12: score, band, percentile rank with reference group and date, and the cut. What would you leave out, and why?
  5. Another reference group. Add kohlmann_2016_phq9, PHQ-9 responses from patients with coronary heart disease (Kohlmann et al., 2016). Where does a 10 fall there? Which reference group should a cardiologist use for a new patient, and for which question?
  6. Challenge (open): prevalence from a screener. The cut of 10 was chosen against diagnostic interviews. Should a population survey report the share scoring 10 or more as the prevalence of depression? Think about the positive predictive value at a low base rate. I don’t know of an agreed answer.

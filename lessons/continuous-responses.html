What this is for

Some items offer no categories: the respondent marks a point on a line, or drags a slider from 0 to 100. Factor analysis II imagined a continuous latent response behind every ordered category; a line seems to hand it to us directly. So do we just fit a factor model? That rests on assumptions about how respondents use the line, and this lesson asks what they are and how the data can check them. But a line has ends, and respondents use them: on one climate-risk slider, almost a quarter of the ratings sit at 100. We build the linear model and Samejima’s continuous response model, look at models that treat the ends as part of the response, and ask what cutting a line into categories costs. On five impulsivity items marked on a line, the graded model fitted to the cut line reports more information than the continuous models, which it can’t have if they hold. Checking that claim takes up most of the real-data section.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • write the linear model for continuous item responses, and say why its information, \(\lambda^2/\psi\), is the same at every \(\theta\);
  • fit Samejima’s continuous response model as a factor model for logits, and convert its estimates to EstCRM’s;
  • say what the ends of a bounded scale do to these models, and what a squeeze constant, a beta model and an ordered beta model each assume about them;
  • compute what cutting a line into \(K\) categories costs, and where;
  • check a model’s claim about information against the data, with a split into halves that doesn’t depend on the model.

Core ideas

The latent response, observed

Recall from Factor analysis II: an ordered response can be read as a latent response \(y^* = \lambda\eta + \varepsilon\) cut at thresholds, and the ordinal factor model that results is the graded response model.

On a line there is nothing to cut. A respondent’s mark \(x_{ij}\), in millimetres or slider points, can be modelled directly: \[x_{ij} = \mu_i + \lambda_i\theta_j + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad \varepsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \psi_i),\] with \(\theta_j\) standard normal. This is the one-factor model of Factor analysis I, with \(\psi_i\) the unique variance, read as an item response model for continuous responses (Mellenbergh, 1994; Bejar, 1977, applies such a model to personality items).

How much does one response say about \(\theta\)? Given \(\theta\), the response is normal with mean \(\mu_i + \lambda_i\theta\) and variance \(\psi_i\), so its log likelihood is \[\ell_i(\theta) = -\frac{(x_{ij} - \mu_i - \lambda_i\theta)^2}{2\psi_i} + \text{const},\] a parabola in \(\theta\). Information is the expected curvature of the log likelihood, \(-E[\ell_i''(\theta)]\). Differentiating twice gives \(\ell_i''(\theta) = -\lambda_i^2/\psi_i\), so the item’s information is \(\lambda_i^2/\psi_i\). Neither \(\theta\) nor the response appears in it, for two reasons. The mean of the response is a straight line in \(\theta\), so a step in \(\theta\) moves it by \(\lambda_i\) everywhere. And the noise around that mean, \(\psi_i\), is the same at every \(\theta\). For a 0/1 item neither holds: the curve is steepest near \(b\) and flat far from it, and the variance \(P(1-P)\) changes with \(\theta\). That is why its information has a peak.

Recall from Information, precision, and short forms: the conditional SEM is \(1/\sqrt{I(\theta)}\), and it replaced the single standard error of measurement of Classical test theory and reliability with one that varies with \(\theta\).

Under the linear model the single SEM comes back: \(1/\sqrt{\sum_i \lambda_i^2/\psi_i}\) for everyone. With standardized loadings, \(\psi_i = 1 - \lambda_i^2\), and a test information of \(I\) gives a reliability of \(I/(1+I)\).

The ends, and Samejima’s model

A line has no ends, but the responses we collect on one usually do: the mark falls on a 112 mm line printed on the page, or a slider stops at 0 and 100. The linear model doesn’t know about these bounds: its mean keeps rising past the top of the scale, and its error variance is the same at the ends as in the middle. Samejima (1973) extended her graded model to a continuous response model. In the parameterization of Wang and Zeng (1998) it is easy to fit: divide by the scale’s maximum \(M\), take logits, \[z_{ij} = \ln\frac{x_{ij}}{M - x_{ij}},\] and fit the linear model to \(z_{ij}\). The logit stretches the ends out to \(\pm\infty\), so the responses stay bounded, and are squeezed near the ends. The EstCRM package (Zopluoglu, 2012) fits it by EM; factanal() on the logits gives the same estimates, as the real-data section shows.

At the defaults the item sits low on the scale for most respondents (\(b = -1\)). The linear band spills over the top for 30% of them, while Samejima’s responses bunch below 1: their SD falls from 0.139 at \(\theta = b\) to 0.069 two units above it. Move \(b\) to 0 and the linear band still leaves the scale for 18%, in both tails.

The widget’s last number is the odd one. Samejima’s responses change shape along \(\theta\), yet the information is flat. That follows from a general fact: Fisher information is unchanged by a one-to-one transformation of the data, because the densities of \(x\) and of \(z\) differ only by a Jacobian that doesn’t involve \(\theta\) (problem 1). The IRW’s bounded continuous responses vignette makes the same point. So Samejima’s model keeps the single SEM.

If you’ve done Response times: the lognormal model for response times is the linear model for log time. Samejima’s model is the same construction, with the logit in place of the log.

Bounded models: beta IRT and its relatives

Another route builds the bounds into the distribution. Beta IRT (Noel & Dauvier, 2007) takes the response, rescaled to (0, 1), to be beta-distributed given \(\theta\), with a mean that rises with \(\theta\) and a precision \(\phi\) that sets the spread. Müller (1987) built a Rasch model for continuous ratings, and Ferrando (2001) a nonlinear factor model for them. Under these models information varies with \(\theta\). The IRW vignette fits them to 11 slider tables: on held-out log probability the bounded models beat the linear model on every table, but on the error of a predicted response (RMSE) they didn’t. They pay off when a question needs the shape of the response, such as the chance of a rating at the ceiling.

Try it: three densities at one θ

One item, three models: Samejima’s (logit slope 1.5, residual SD 1), a beta with the same median and precision \(\phi = 10\), and the same beta with shares \(\omega_0\) and \(\omega_1\) of responses exactly at 0 and 1. The shares are set at \(\theta = 0\) and move with \(\theta\) on the logit scale, with slope 1.5.

Responses at the ends

A response of exactly 0 or \(M\) has an infinite logit. Samejima’s model needs the ends pulled in first, \(z = \ln\{(x + \nu)/(M - x + \nu)\}\) with a small squeeze constant \(\nu\). The beta needs the same (Smithson & Verkuilen, 2006, squeeze by an amount that shrinks with the sample size). When few responses sit at the ends, the choice hardly matters. When many do, it matters a good deal: Molenaar, Cúri and Bazán (2022) found estimates affected with as few as 5% of responses on a bound.

With \(\nu = 0.5\) it sits just past the other logits; with \(\nu = 0.005\) it stands far out, and the fitted information falls from 4.01 to 2.54, against 4.50 with no ceiling. Move the items down to 0 and the three agree within 0.25. The squeeze constant decides where to put responses the model can’t place.

Two families give the ends their own probabilities instead. Zero-one inflated models add a share at each end to a continuous density (Ospina & Ferrari, 2010), and Molenaar, Cúri and Bazán (2022) build such shares into four IRT models for bounded responses. They allow responses at the ends to arise differently from the rest, for example as a choice of the end itself. The ordered beta (Kubinec, 2023) uses cutpoints, as ordered logit does, to fit one linear model to 0, to 1 and to the beta-distributed responses in between. It treats a respondent at 100 as one who would rate higher still. Both can be fitted with glmmTMB (Brooks et al., 2017).

Cutting the line

Recall from Models for polytomous responses: merging two categories never adds information (the Cauchy–Schwarz step in its second Go deeper callout), and collapsing a four-category item costs most away from the boundary that is kept.

A line is the limit of that argument. If the linear model holds, a line cut into \(K\) categories is an ordinal item with its thresholds at the cuts, and it can’t carry more than the line’s \(\lambda^2/\psi\). MacCallum, Zhang, Preacher and Rucker (2002) make the parallel case about dichotomizing a continuous variable. How much does a cut item carry?

NoteGo deeper: the line as the limit of the graded model, and why cutting it loses information

Assumptions: one item; the response (or its logit) is \(y = \mu + \lambda\theta + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, \psi)\); item parameters known.

Cutting gives a graded item. Cut \(y\) at thresholds \(t_1 < \dots < t_{K-1}\). The chance of reaching category \(k\) or above is \(\Pr(y > t_k \mid \theta) = \Phi\big((\mu + \lambda\theta - t_k)/\sqrt\psi\big)\): a normal-ogive graded item with slope \(\lambda/\sqrt\psi\) and boundaries \((t_k - \mu)/\lambda\).

The limit. Let the thresholds fill the line, spaced \(h\) apart, with \(h \to 0\). Category \(k\)’s probability becomes \(h\,f(t_k \mid \theta)\), with \(f\) the normal density of \(y\) given \(\theta\), and the polytomous information \(\sum_k (P_k')^2/P_k\) becomes \[\int \frac{\big(\partial f(y \mid \theta)/\partial\theta\big)^2}{f(y \mid \theta)}\,dy = \frac{\lambda^2}{\psi},\] the Fisher information about a normal mean \(\mu + \lambda\theta\) with variance \(\psi\). With the logit of the response as \(y\), this is Samejima’s model.

Cutting loses information. Any cut version is a merger of the fine categories. By the Cauchy–Schwarz step in Models for polytomous responses, each merger keeps or lowers the information, so a cut item has at most \(\lambda^2/\psi\) at every \(\theta\), with equality only in the limit.

What the proof needs. Both steps assume the model holds. If the response isn’t normal given \(\theta\), then \(\lambda^2/\psi\) is what a normal model reports, not necessarily the information in the data, and a graded model fitted to the cut line can report more. The real-data section meets exactly this.

Do sliders behave like items with more categories?

That depends on how respondents use them. A respondent who picks only multiples of 10 is answering an 11-category item. In a web experiment by Couper, Tourangeau, Conrad and Singer (2006), visual analogue scales gave the same response distributions as radio buttons, with more missing data and longer completion times. In one by Funke and Reips (2012), respondents adjusted their ratings more often on the line than on five-point scales, with no difference in means, nonresponse or time. Matejka, Glueck, Grossman and Fitzmaurice (2016) found that a slider’s ticks and labels shift where respondents put it. Preston and Colman (2000) found reliability and validity rising with the number of categories up to about seven. Heaping can be counted: an evenly spread 0–100 response lands on a multiple of 10 about 11% of the time, and the vignette found a table where over half the responses sat on the scale’s quarter points. A paper line scored with a ruler gives respondents no numbers to round to.

Simulate

The code simulates 500 respondents answering six slider items from Samejima’s model, fits the linear model, Samejima’s model (\(\nu = 0.5\)) and the graded model on K bins, and compares each with the true test information. The first run takes 20–30 seconds while R and mirt load.

With the seed as written, the true test information is 5.2; the linear model gives 4.7 and Samejima’s 5.6. The graded model on five bins gives 5.6 at \(\theta = 0\) and 1.6 at \(\theta = -2\). That 5.6 is above the truth, which a cut line can’t carry: sampling error, and perhaps the graded model’s logistic link where these data are normal. Keep it in mind. Set ends <- 0.1 to push responses to the ends: information falls to 3.3 (linear) and 2.5 (Samejima). Heaping, round_to <- 10, costs little (4.7 for both).

With real data

Impulsivity on a line

Our main example is estcrm_epia: 1,033 undergraduates each marked five items of the impulsivity subscale of the Spanish Eysenck Personality Inventory (EPI-A) on a 112 mm line running from “almost never” to “almost always”, scored in millimetres from 1 to 111 (Ferrando, 2002; distributed as EPIA in EstCRM, Zopluoglu, 2012). Its job here: a line that respondents use away from its ends, where the continuous models should do well. Higher means more impulsive, and all five items are keyed that way. A check that leans on neither: split the five items into a pair and a triple (10 ways) and correlate the halves’ sums. More information means halves that agree more, and each fitted model says how much they should.

# A check that doesn't trust either model: split the five items into two halves (a

  B <- setdiff(1:5, A); cor(rowSums(scale(Y[, A])), rowSums(scale(Y[, B])))

What does the choice among these models turn on? Two assumptions: that the response is normal given \(\theta\) (on the raw or the logit scale), and that responses at the ends are ordinary responses that happen to be extreme. On the EPI lines, marks sit away from the ends, the two continuous models agree, and the linear model’s claim about the halves matches the data. In the case that responses sit away from the ends and the goal is a score, the linear factor model on the raw responses may be sufficient for some purposes. In the case that a fifth or more of the responses sit at an end, as on the climate slider, a model that gives the ends their own probability, such as the ordered beta, is the one whose predictions at the ends can be checked against the data. And in the case that a slider is to be cut, five categories holding equal shares may be sufficient for some purposes: under the linear model they keep about nine tenths of the information in the middle and two thirds two SDs out. Where these assumptions are in doubt, the vignette’s comparison on held-out responses can be run on any of these models.

Problems

  1. Information on two scales. (a) Show that the linear model’s item information is \(\lambda^2/\psi\) at every \(\theta\). (b) Show that Fisher information about \(\theta\) is unchanged by a one-to-one, differentiable transformation of the response that doesn’t depend on \(\theta\). (c) Show that EstCRM’s \(a\) equals \(\lambda/\sqrt{1 - \lambda^2}\), with \(\lambda\) the standardized loading of the logits.
  2. Where does the scale end? estcrm_selfeff has 307 pre-service teachers’ marks on ten teaching self-efficacy items, made on an 11 cm line (Kan, 2009; EstCRM::SelfEff). Many marks fall between 9.5 and 10.5 cm, and a few above. Fit Samejima’s model with the scale maximum at 11 and at 10.5 (squeezing the marks above 10.5). What moves, and why? Which maximum would you defend?
  3. Replacing a five-point item. A team plans to replace a five-point item with a 0–100 slider “to get more information”. What would you look for in pilot data (heaping, the ends, the information by \(K\) from this lesson’s code) before agreeing, and what result would make the switch worth it?
  4. Designing a slider. Design a slider item to limit heaping and piling at the ends. Decide whether it shows numbers, where the handle starts and how the ends are labelled, drawing on Couper et al. (2006) and Matejka et al. (2016). How would you test your design against a five-point version?
  5. Does imagery move the ends? Use wave 2 of karlsson_2023_climate_risk and the three arms in cov_condition. Fit the ordered beta with the arm as a predictor. Does the manipulation move the share at 100, the middle of the scale, or both? What does the local dependence within pairs do to your standard errors?
  6. Challenge (open): the graded model’s extra information. On the EPI lines the graded model fitted to bins reports about 40% more information than the linear model, yet the halves agree only as much as the linear model implies. Why does the graded model report more? Test one explanation: error flatter than normal, a variance that shrinks near the ends, a beta response, or the logistic link. I don’t know which one holds.

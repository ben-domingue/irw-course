What this is for

A collection of calibrated items is an item bank, and its most demanding use is computerized adaptive testing (CAT): each item is chosen after the last answer, to suit the respondent. Information, precision, and short forms picked the most informative items for one point on the scale; a CAT does that again after every response, at the respondent’s current estimate. This lesson builds the loop from information and the EAP, asks what stopping rules buy and what choosing by information costs, and runs adaptive tests after the fact on a medical examination and the PROMIS depression items. A CAT is only as good as the bank is where the respondent sits.

Recall from What is measurement?: one of the things we want from a measure is that it give precise information quickly, with few items. Adaptive testing is the most direct attempt at “quickly”.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • say what an item bank holds and what keeping one up involves;
  • run the adaptive loop: start, choose by information, score by EAP, stop;
  • compare fixed-length, standard-error and classification stopping rules;
  • explain what maximum information does to item exposure, and what the fixes cost;
  • run a post-hoc CAT on real responses and read it as a statement about the bank.

Core ideas

An item bank

What does a bank store? At the least, each item and its calibrated parameters, fixed, so that a respondent’s \(\theta\) can be estimated from any subset of the items and lands on the bank’s scale (Wainer et al., 2000; van der Linden & Glas, 2010). A working bank also records each item’s content codes, dates, how often it has been given, and the checks it has passed.

Recall from The Rasch model: outfit and infit are near 1 if the model is right; well below 1 an item overfits (it is more predictable than the model expects), well above 1 it underfits. A bank screens its items with statistics like these.

If you’ve done Model fit and out-of-sample prediction, outfit’s spread under a true model shrinks with sample size, so the screening rule should depend on the calibration sample. If you’ve done Differential item functioning, each item also gets a DIF screen, which needs group memberships in the calibration data. If you’ve done Linking and equating, new items join the bank’s scale by calibration alongside old ones acting as anchors. A bank is maintained, not finished: overused items are retired, old items checked for drift, new ones added.

The adaptive loop

Recall from Information, precision, and short forms: a 2PL item’s information is \(a^2P(1-P)\), largest at \(\theta = b\); test information adds across items, and the conditional SEM is \(1/\sqrt{I(\theta)}\).

How do we choose items for someone whose \(\theta\) we don’t yet know? One at a time (Lord, 1980, ch. 10; Weiss, 1982):

  1. Start at the population mean.
  2. Choose the unused item most informative at the current estimate, and give it.
  3. Update the estimate from every response so far.
  4. Stop when a rule says so; otherwise go back to 2.

Step 3 has a problem at the start.

Recall from Estimating abilities: an all-right or all-wrong pattern has no maximum-likelihood estimate; the EAP, the posterior mean under a normal prior, exists for every pattern.

After one item every pattern is all-right or all-wrong, so there is no MLE until the respondent has one of each; the EAP exists throughout, and adaptive testing and Bayesian scoring grew up together (Owen, 1975; Bock & Mislevy, 1982). Adapting is older than computers: Binet and Simon (1904) graded their tasks by the age at which children pass them, and individually given tests still start at a basal level and stop at a ceiling.

NoteGo deeper: maximum information and the posterior

Assumptions: known item parameters; local independence; EAP scoring with a normal prior. Write \(p_k(\theta)\) for the posterior after \(k\) items, with mean \(\hat\theta_k\) and variance \(\sigma_k^2\).

What we would like to minimize. If a CAT stops on the posterior SD, a natural choice of next item \(j\) is the one that leaves the smallest posterior variance after its response, averaged over the responses it might get: \[\text{EPV}_j = \sum_{x} \Pr(x_j = x \mid \text{responses so far})\; \text{Var}(\theta \mid \text{responses so far},\, x_j = x),\] where \(\Pr(x_j = x \mid \cdot) = \int \Pr(x_j = x \mid \theta)\,p_k(\theta)\,d\theta\). Owen (1975) selected items with a normal approximation to the posterior; van der Linden (1998) sets out selection criteria that use the posterior itself.

Why maximum information approximates it. Suppose the posterior is close to normal and narrow enough that item \(j\)’s information barely changes across it. Then one more response adds about \(I_j(\hat\theta_k)\) to the posterior’s precision, as a normal likelihood carrying that information would: \[\frac{1}{\sigma_{k+1}^2} \approx \frac{1}{\sigma_k^2} + I_j(\hat\theta_k).\] The right-hand side increases with \(I_j(\hat\theta_k)\), so the item with the most information at the EAP is, approximately, the item that shrinks the posterior most.

When they part. Early in the test the posterior is wide (\(\sigma_0 = 1\)), and a steep item’s information is concentrated in a band much narrower than that: an item with \(a = 2.4\) has lost about 70% of its peak information one logit from its \(b\). What matters then is information averaged over where \(\theta\) might be, \(\int I_j(\theta)\,p_k(\theta)\,d\theta\), which can favour a flatter item near the posterior’s centre. Chang and Ying (1996) argue that information at the estimate is least suited to the start of a test, and propose a global (Kullback–Leibler) information for the early items. As responses accumulate, the posterior narrows, the approximation improves, and the criteria agree.

Stopping rules

When should a CAT stop? Three answers are standard (Weiss & Kingsbury, 1984).

  • Fixed length. Everyone gets, say, 20 items: predictable time, easy content balance, precision that varies along the scale.
  • Target standard error. Stop when the posterior SD reaches, say, 0.3, with a cap on length: equal precision wherever the bank can deliver it, and varying length.
  • Classification. For a pass/fail decision, stop once the responses favour one side of the cut clearly enough (callout below). If you’ve done What does a score mean?, this spends the items near the cut, where classification is uncertain.
NoteGo deeper: stopping for a pass/fail decision

Assumptions: known item parameters; local independence; one cut \(\theta_c\) and an indifference region \(\theta_c \pm \delta\) within which either decision is acceptable; tolerated error rates \(\alpha\) (failing someone at \(\theta_c + \delta\)) and \(\beta\) (passing someone at \(\theta_c - \delta\)).

The test. After \(k\) items, compare the likelihood of the responses at the two edges of the region, \[\Lambda_k = \frac{L(\theta_c + \delta \mid x_1, \dots, x_k)}{L(\theta_c - \delta \mid x_1, \dots, x_k)}.\] Wald’s (1945) sequential probability ratio test stops and passes the respondent once \(\Lambda_k \ge (1 - \beta)/\alpha\), stops and fails them once \(\Lambda_k \le \beta/(1 - \alpha)\), and otherwise gives another item. With \(\alpha = \beta = 0.05\) the bounds are 19 and 1/19.

Which items. Every item moves \(\log\Lambda\) by \(\log[P_i(\theta_c+\delta)/P_i(\theta_c-\delta)]\) if right and by the corresponding ratio of \(1 - P_i\) if wrong, so the items that move it most sit near the cut, whatever the current estimate. Choosing by information at the cut is the simple rule; Eggen (1999) compares it with Kullback–Leibler information, and Spray and Reckase (1996) compare the SPRT with a Bayesian sequential rule. Respondents far from the cut are classified in a few items; those near it take the longest, and a cap decides what happens to them. Problem 5 tries it on the CMSCE.

Recall from Estimating abilities: the EAP is pulled toward the prior mean, most where the likelihood is flattest, and its standard error is the posterior SD.

A standard-error rule stops on the posterior SD, and the posterior includes the prior: where the bank is thin, part of the precision it counts is the prior’s, and the estimate that stops has been pulled toward the mean. (Between fixed forms and a CAT sit multistage tests, which adapt between short preassembled modules; Luecht & Nungester, 1998.)

What maximum information costs

Choosing the most informative item is efficient, with side effects (Kingsbury & Zara, 1989). Everyone sees the same first item and respondents who answer alike see the same next ones, so a few steep items go to almost everyone; where a test has stakes, such items become known. And information knows nothing about content: a CAT on a medical examination could spend every item on one specialty unless told otherwise.

The fixes trade precision for control (Revuelta & Ponsoda, 1998, compare eight). Randomesque selection picks at random among the few most informative items (Kingsbury & Zara, 1989). Sympson–Hetter gives each selected item a probability of being administered, tuned by simulation to cap its exposure (Hetter & Sympson, 1997; van der Linden, 2003). a-stratification splits the bank by slope and moves from the flattest stratum to the steepest, matching \(b\) to the estimate within each (Chang & Ying, 1999; Chang, 2015).

Everything rests on the bank’s parameters

A CAT puts every respondent’s different test on one scale, which works only if the bank’s parameters, estimated from particular respondents taking a particular form, hold for other respondents and other combinations of items. If you’ve done Parameter invariance, that lesson tested this property. The ROAR-CAT project checked it before going live (Ma et al., 2025): it calibrated a reading bank in four groups of students, removed six items that functioned differently across them, and then randomly assigned students to random or CAT order. To reach a reliability of 0.9, 75 CAT items did the work of 125 random ones.

A cheaper check uses data already in hand: a post-hoc CAT. Calibrate on some respondents who answered every item, and let a CAT choose items for the others, revealing only the responses it asks for; then compare its estimate with the whole bank’s, respondent by respondent. The unchosen items are missing by design, which is ignorable given the model (Mislevy & Wu, 1996). What it can’t show is whether respondents answer an item the same way inside a CAT as inside the fixed form.

Code 
# A CAT in base R, for any unidimensional model mirt fits. Every quantity lives on a
# grid of nodes: the prior, each item's response probabilities and its information.
nodes <- seq(-6, 6, length.out = 121)
prior <- dnorm(nodes)   # mirt's calibration fixes the mean at 0 and the SD at 1
make_bank <- function(model, items) {
  it <- lapply(items, function(i) extract.item(model, i))
  list(items = items,
       logp = lapply(it, function(e) log(probtrace(e, nodes))),  # nodes x categories
       info = sapply(it, function(e) iteminfo(e, nodes)))         # nodes x items
}
# EAP and posterior SD from the log likelihood on the nodes
eap <- function(ll) {
  w <- exp(ll - max(ll)) * prior
  w <- w / sum(w)
  th <- sum(nodes * w)
  c(theta = th, se = sqrt(sum((nodes - th)^2 * w)))
}
# Each available item's information at theta, interpolated between nodes
info_at <- function(bank, th, avail) {
  k <- findInterval(th, nodes, all.inside = TRUE)
  f <- (th - nodes[k]) / (nodes[k + 1] - nodes[k])
  (1 - f) * bank$info[k, avail] + f * bank$info[k + 1, avail]
}
# One respondent. x holds their responses (0, 1, ...) to the bank items, in bank order.
# Start at the prior mean; give the most informative item at the current EAP (or pick
# at random among the `top` most informative, or at random from the whole bank); update;
# stop at max_items or when the posterior SD reaches se_stop.
run_cat <- function(x, bank, max_items = 20, se_stop = 0, select = "info", top = 1) {
  ll <- numeric(length(nodes))
  used <- integer(0)
  est <- eap(ll)
  while (length(used) < max_items && est[["se"]] > se_stop) {
    avail <- setdiff(seq_along(bank$items), used)
    if (select == "random") {
      j <- avail[sample.int(length(avail), 1)]
    } else {
      best <- avail[order(info_at(bank, est[["theta"]], avail), decreasing = TRUE)]
      best <- best[seq_len(min(top, length(best)))]
      j <- best[sample.int(length(best), 1)]
    }
    ll <- ll + bank$logp[[j]][, x[j] + 1]
    used <- c(used, j)
    est <- eap(ll)
  }
  list(theta = est[["theta"]], se = est[["se"]], n = length(used), used = used)
}
bank_cm <- make_bank(m_cm, bank_items)

Choi et al. (2025) saw the same in their live CAT, where students at the extremes reached its 50-item maximum, and they call for more hard items. The post-hoc CAT finds this before anyone is tested. What does the choice of stopping rule rest on? Two assumptions: whether scores must be compared at equal precision, and whether everyone must get the same testing time. A target standard error with a cap takes the first and gives up the second; a fixed length does the reverse. A post-hoc CAT on real responses, as here, shows what each rule costs for a given bank, and where the bank runs out before any respondent does. For this bank, the students above \(\theta = 2\) reach the 100-item cap with a posterior SD of 0.37, which says that new hard items would buy more than a higher cap.

PROMIS depression: a bank built for symptoms

For contrast, promis1wave1_depression: the 56 candidate depression items from the first wave of PROMIS testing, given to US adults (Cella et al., 2010; data at https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/0NGAKG). Its job is to show a bank whose information sits where its purpose puts it. The items ask how often, in the past seven days, the respondent had a depressive feeling or thought, from never to always; PROMIS kept 28 in its bank (Pilkonis et al., 2011). HealthMeasures, which distributes PROMIS, doesn’t permit republishing the items, so we don’t quote them. Higher means more frequent symptoms; we recode the categories 0–4.

Recall from Models for polytomous responses: the graded response model gives each category boundary a 2PL with the item’s slope, so a five-category item is informative near each of its four boundaries. PROMIS calibrated its banks with it.

Problems

  1. Which item next? (a) Under the Rasch model, show that the most informative item at \(\hat\theta\) has \(b\) closest to \(\hat\theta\). (b) Under the 2PL, which of \(a = 2, b = 1\) and \(a = 1, b = 0\) is more informative at \(\hat\theta = 0\), and where does the answer change? Why does maximum information give steep items early?
  2. A stricter bank. Apply Choi et al.’s (2025) screen (slopes above 0.6, \(|b| < 5\)) to the CMSCE calibration and rerun the target-SE CAT on the 50 live respondents. How much bank is left, and what happens at the top?
  3. You work at the state agency. You have last year’s summative test data for one grade: every student’s scored responses to a fixed form, and nothing else about the students. Build an item bank from it: which fields does each item’s record need, how would you calibrate, and how would next year’s new items join? Then say what you can’t do with these data, and what you would ask for. (There are no data for this problem.)
  4. The low end. A general-population survey expects most respondents to report few depressive symptoms. Would you use the PROMIS CAT, a fixed short form, or new low-end items first, and how would you calibrate new items onto the bank’s scale?
  5. Pass or fail. Put a pass mark at the 10th percentile of the 600 CMSCE students’ full-bank \(\theta\) and stop with an SPRT (\(\delta = 0.2\), error rates of 5%), choosing items by information at the cut. How many items does a decision take, by band, against the target-SE rule, and how many students are classified differently from their full-bank \(\theta\)?
  6. Challenge (open): post-hoc and live. A post-hoc CAT reuses responses given in a fixed form and order. How might responses in a live CAT differ (item position, fatigue, a test that feels hard to strong respondents, stakes)? Design a study that would show whether post-hoc results carry over. I don’t think one study settles it for every bank.

What this is for

The Rasch model gives every item the same slope, and the chess data have already turned up an item that doesn’t go along: problem Y15, about as hard as Y29, whose success barely moves with skill. What happens when each item gets its own slope, and then its own floor and ceiling? This lesson builds that family, from the 1PL to the 4PL, asks what each new parameter costs and what the data can say about it, and fits all four to the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test. For that test my choice is the 2PL, and most of the lesson is the case for it.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • write down the 4PL, say what \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) and \(u\) do to a curve, and which constraints give the 3PL, 2PL and Rasch model;
  • explain why the weighted score \(\sum_i a_i x_i\) replaces the sum score under the 2PL, and why crossing curves give up specific objectivity;
  • convert mirt’s intercept to a difficulty, and say how each model fixes the unit of the scale;
  • explain why a lower asymptote is hard to estimate, and when a 3PL is a reasonable choice;
  • fit all four models with mirt and compare them by BIC and by what they say about respondents.

Core ideas

One family of curves

One formula covers every model here. For person \(j\) and item \(i\), \[\Pr(x_{ij} = 1) = c_i + (u_i - c_i)\,\frac{\exp\big(a_i(\theta_j - b_i)\big)}{1 + \exp\big(a_i(\theta_j - b_i)\big)}.\]

  • \(b_i\) is the item’s difficulty, its location on the \(\theta\) scale.
  • \(a_i\) is its slope or discrimination: how quickly the probability rises as \(\theta\) passes \(b_i\).
  • \(c_i\) is the lower asymptote, the probability of success at very low ability; on multiple-choice items it is called the guessing parameter.
  • \(u_i\) is the upper asymptote; below 1, even the strongest respondents sometimes miss (a “slip”).

“PL” stands for “parameter logistic”, and the number counts the free item parameters. The Rasch model, a 1PL, frees only \(b\) (\(a = 1\), \(c = 0\), \(u = 1\)). The 2PL frees \(a\) and the 3PL frees \(c\) as well, both from Birnbaum’s chapters in Lord & Novick (1968). The 4PL frees \(u\) too (Barton & Lord, 1981; Loken & Rulison, 2010).

What slopes cost: sufficiency and crossing curves

Recall from The Rasch model: under the Rasch model the sum score \(r\) is sufficient for \(\theta\): patterns with the same sum score share an ability estimate. That lesson’s widget broke this by switching to a 2PL.

What takes the sum score’s place under the 2PL? Write out one person’s likelihood, as the Rasch lesson’s Go deeper callout did: \[\Pr(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta) = \prod_i \frac{\exp\big(x_i a_i(\theta - b_i)\big)}{1 + \exp\big(a_i(\theta - b_i)\big)} = \frac{\exp\big(\theta \sum_i a_i x_i\big)\,\exp\big(-\sum_i a_i b_i x_i\big)}{\prod_i \big(1 + \exp(a_i(\theta - b_i))\big)}.\] The data meet \(\theta\) only through the weighted score \(\sum_i a_i x_i\), so under the 2PL it is sufficient (Birnbaum, 1968). A correct answer counts in proportion to its item’s slope. The weights are estimated item parameters, though; Andersen (1977) showed that a sufficient statistic free of the item parameters exists only under the Rasch model. Under the 3PL the lower asymptote spoils the factorization, and no simple statistic is sufficient.

Recall from The Rasch model: specific objectivity asks that comparisons between items not depend on who takes them. With equal slopes, curves never cross, so one item is easier than another for everyone.

Give two items different slopes and their curves cross where \(a_1(\theta - b_1) = a_2(\theta - b_2)\): below that point one item is easier, above it the other. The 2PL buys its better description of items by giving up specific objectivity, and the chess data below show a crossing where most players sit.

NoteGo deeper: the weighted score under the 2PL, and why the 3PL has none

Assumptions: one respondent answers \(I\) dichotomous items; responses are locally independent given \(\theta\); the item parameters are known. Write \(L_i = \text{logit}^{-1}(a_i(\theta - b_i))\).

2PL. The factorization in the main text puts \(\theta\) and the data together only in \(\exp(\theta \sum_i a_i x_i)\), so by the factorization theorem \(t = \sum_i a_i x_i\) is sufficient. The score equation says the same thing: differentiating the log likelihood, \[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} = \sum_i a_i\,(x_i - L_i) = t - \sum_i a_i L_i,\] so the MLE solves \(\sum_i a_i L_i(\theta) = t\), which involves the data only through \(t\). Conditioning on \(t\) removes \(\theta\), just as conditioning on \(r\) did for the Rasch model. It buys little here: with slopes that are real numbers, nearly every pattern has its own weighted score, so the conditional distribution given \(t\) usually has one pattern in it and nothing to estimate from.

3PL. Now \(P_i = c_i + (1 - c_i) L_i\), and \(\partial P_i/\partial\theta = a_i (P_i - c_i)(1 - P_i)/(1 - c_i)\). The same calculation gives \[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} = \sum_i a_i\,(x_i - P_i)\,\frac{P_i - c_i}{P_i\,(1 - c_i)}.\] With \(c_i = 0\) the last fraction is 1 and we’re back to the 2PL. With \(c_i > 0\) it is a weight that depends on \(\theta\): near 1 for a person well above the item, near 0 for a person well below it, where a correct answer is mostly chance. Each correct answer’s weight therefore changes with the ability at which it is evaluated, and no single function of the pattern carries all the information about \(\theta\). That is the widget’s 3PL result: a lone correct answer can leave the estimate at the bottom of the scale.

Reading mirt, and what fixes the scale

mirt (Chalmers, 2012) writes these models with an intercept, \(\Pr(x = 1) = g + (u - g)\,\text{logit}^{-1}(a\theta + d)\); its g is our \(c\) and its u is our \(u\). Matching \(a\theta + d\) with \(a(\theta - b)\) gives \(b = -d/a\), the conversion every chunk below uses. When \(a\) is near 0, that ratio can be enormous, as the chess data will show.

What fixes the unit of \(\theta\)? The model only sees \(a(\theta - b)\), so multiplying every \(\theta\) and \(b\) by \(k\) and dividing every \(a\) by \(k\) changes no probability. The unit is a convention the software sets, as the origin was (The Rasch model): the Rasch fit fixes the slopes at 1 and estimates the SD of \(\theta\); the 2PL fixes that SD at 1. A Rasch fit and a 1PL with one common, estimated slope are then the same model reporting different \(a\) and \(b\). That matters when reading output or comparing numbers across fits, the problem Linking and equating takes up, but it says nothing about the data.

What the lower asymptote needs

The lower asymptote describes people far below an item’s difficulty. If few respondents are down there, the data say little about it, and a 2PL, with a lower slope and a shifted difficulty, can imitate a 3PL where the respondents are.

Slide the mean ability down to −2 and the largest difference grows eight-fold, to 0.08: now many respondents sit near the floor, and the floor matters.

Domingue et al. (2024) looked at this through prediction. They simulated data from the 3PL and asked how much better a fitted 3PL predicted new responses than a fitted 2PL, using the IMV, a measure of predictive gain that Model fit and out-of-sample prediction teaches. The average gain never exceeded 0.001, even with pronounced guessing in the generating model, and across 89 IRW datasets it averaged 0.00016. They connect this to the difficulty of identifying the 3PL’s parameters (Maris & Bechger, 2009; Haberman, 2005; von Davier, 2009), noting that 3PL estimates may still flag items with guessing problems. A 3PL is a reasonable choice when the items are multiple choice, when a good share of respondents sits near chance, when the sample is large, or when \(c\) is fixed or given a prior, as in Guessing and priors.

The upper asymptote: ceilings and slips

What does \(u < 1\) mean? That respondents far above an item’s difficulty still sometimes miss it: through carelessness, a slip, or an item harder than it looks for experts. Barton and Lord (1981) added the upper asymptote to the 3PL in an ETS report; Loken and Rulison (2010) estimated the 4PL with Bayesian methods and applied it to a delinquency scale.

The ceiling mirrors the floor: estimating \(u\) needs many respondents far above the item, missing it now and then. Most data have few, so \(u\) is weakly identified. On the RMET, the 4PL had not converged when EM stopped at its 500-cycle limit (below).

The same two asymptotes return, without a curve between them, in cognitive diagnosis. In the DINA model (Junker & Sijtsma, 2001), a respondent who has every attribute an item requires answers correctly with probability \(1 - s\), where \(s\) is the item’s slip parameter, and a respondent who lacks any of them with probability \(g\), its guess. Cognitive diagnostic models, an extension lesson, takes this up.

Two philosophies

The choice between these models is also a choice between two traditions, which Andrich (2004) describes as incompatible paradigms in Kuhn’s sense. What does each assume?

  • The model comes first. The Rasch tradition starts from what measurement should mean: a sufficient sum score, and comparisons that don’t depend on which items or people were used (Rasch, 1960; Wright, 1997). It assumes items can be revised until they fit, and treats misfit as information about the items. It suits building an instrument you can still change.
  • The data come first. The tradition that runs from Birnbaum through Lord (1980) takes the items as given and adds parameters until the curves describe them, accepting weighted scores and crossing curves. It suits analysing a test you can’t change.

Some of the disagreement is about values, which data can’t settle; some is empirical, and the RMET tests it below.

Beyond four: asymmetric curves

All four models give point-symmetric curves. Samejima (2000) proposed the logistic positive exponent family, which raises a logistic curve to a positive power so that it can rise slowly and then steeply, or the reverse. Lee and Bolt (2018) study what asymmetry can say about item complexity, and the IRW’s asymmetric models vignette fits such models across its tables.

With real data

Our main example is wilmer-rmet-normative-data-set-2022, responses to the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test (RMET; Baron-Cohen et al., 2001), a measure of reading mental states from the eye region of a face. BIC (Schwarz, 1978) adds a penalty for each parameter to minus twice the log likelihood; smaller is better. It prefers the 2PL (204,337) to the Rasch model (205,192, or 855 higher), the 3PL (204,617) and the 4PL (204,707). The 4PL hadn’t converged at EM’s 500-cycle limit, itself a sign that the data say little about four parameters per item. So which model would I fit to a test like the RMET? The 2PL. The slopes differ six-fold, BIC prefers it to the Rasch model by 855 points, the lower asymptote can’t be estimated from a sample with almost no one near chance, and the richer models’ abilities correlate with the 2PL’s at 0.987 or above. If the RMET were being revised, the Rasch tradition’s question (which items to rewrite so that one slope fits them all) would be a fair one; for the test as it stands, the 2PL describes it.

Problems

  1. Weighted scores. Show that under the 2PL, \(\sum_i a_i x_i\) is sufficient for \(\theta\). Then, for the 3PL, find two patterns on three items of your choosing with the same \(\sum_i a_i x_i\) but different maximum-likelihood estimates.
  2. What \(b\) means now. For the 3PL, show that at \(\theta = b\) the probability is \((1 + c)/2\). At what \(\theta\) is the probability 0.5? What does \(b\) describe, in words, once \(c > 0\)? And for the 4PL?
  3. Where the floor is. Fit the 2PL and the 3PL to the RMET using only the respondents in the bottom quarter of sum scores. Do the estimated lower asymptotes change? Explain what you see, and why selecting on the same items’ sum score might itself cause trouble.
  4. Reading \(c\). A colleague wants to report the RMET’s 3PL lower asymptotes as “the rate at which people guess on each item”. What would you tell them, using this lesson’s output?
  5. Crossing curves. In the chess data, find two problems other than Y15 whose 2PL curves cross where most players sit. For which players is each the easier one? Check against grouped proportions, as the lesson did for Y15 and Y29.
  6. Challenge: when does the model matter? The four models’ RMET abilities correlate at 0.97 or above. When would the choice still matter for a decision about one person? Think about the ends of the scale, cut scores and unusual patterns; Loken & Rulison (2010) is one place to start. This one is open: I don’t know of a general answer.

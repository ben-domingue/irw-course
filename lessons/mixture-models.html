What this is for

Every model so far has assumed one measurement model for every respondent. What if some respondents answer a different question, or none at all? On an online extraversion survey analysed below, about half the respondents from one platform answer as if the content of the items didn’t matter, and their sum scores are exactly as high as everyone else’s. A mixture model lets respondents belong to latent classes, each with its own measurement model, and asks the data who is in which. Guessing and priors fitted the simplest one. This lesson takes the idea to inattentive responding and response styles, and asks when a class is a kind of respondent and when it merely patches the model.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • write a pattern’s likelihood under a mixture and compute posterior class probabilities;
  • say when two respondents with the same sum score can have different posteriors;
  • compute the usual screens for careless responding, and explain why they disagree;
  • explain why a content-free class shows on a balanced scale and hides in the trait on an all-forward one;
  • fit mixture Rasch and mixed partial credit models in mirt, choose the number of classes, and check a class against outside evidence.

Core ideas

One population, or several?

Suppose each respondent belongs to one of \(K\) latent classes, with shares \(\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K\) that sum to 1, and each class \(k\) has its own measurement model. In the mixture Rasch model (Rost, 1990; Mislevy & Verhelst, 1990), class \(k\) has its own item difficulties \(b_{ik}\) and its own distribution of \(\theta\). For a respondent whose class we don’t know, the probability of a pattern \(\mathbf{x}\) is a weighted sum over classes, and Bayes’ rule turns it around, as the EAP of Estimating abilities did with a prior over \(\theta\): \[\Pr(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \Pr(\mathbf{x} \mid k), \qquad \Pr(k \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\pi_k \Pr(\mathbf{x} \mid k)}{\sum_{m} \pi_m \Pr(\mathbf{x} \mid m)}.\]

Recall from Guessing and priors: in the “safety valve” of Xiao et al. (2026), a share \(\pi\) of respondents follow the Rasch model and the rest answer at chance; on roar_lexical, \(\hat\pi\) was 0.87. One class’s model is fixed in advance.

Here neither class is fixed. On a survey, the second class might be a content-free class: respondents who agree (or disagree) whatever an item says.

Recall from The Rasch model: the sum score is sufficient for \(\theta\), and item comparisons don’t depend on who took the items (specific objectivity).

Both hold within a class, not between classes. Why can the pattern carry the class when the sum can’t? If two classes’ difficulties differ only by a constant, the class posterior depends on the pattern only through the sum (first Go deeper callout): the classes are levels of one continuum. Only when the difficulty profiles cross does the pattern carry the class, and then the classes differ in kind. If you’ve done Latent classes and cognitive diagnosis, classes there were fixed by the attributes mastered; here a class is defined by the model a respondent follows.

NoteGo deeper: why the pattern, not the sum, carries the class

Assumptions: a mixture Rasch model with classes \(k = 1, \dots, K\), shares \(\pi_k\), difficulties \(b_{ik}\) and ability densities \(g_k(\theta)\); local independence within a class.

Within a class. Writing \(r = \sum_i x_i\), \[\Pr(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta, k) = \prod_i \frac{\exp\{x_i(\theta - b_{ik})\}}{1 + \exp(\theta - b_{ik})} = \exp\Big(-\sum_i x_i b_{ik}\Big)\,\frac{\exp(r\theta)}{\prod_i \{1 + \exp(\theta - b_{ik})\}}.\] Integrating over \(g_k\) gives \(\Pr(\mathbf{x} \mid k) = \exp(-\sum_i x_i b_{ik})\,h_k(r)\), where \(h_k(r)\) depends on the pattern only through \(r\) (Rost, 1990, writes the same factorization with each class’s score distribution).

The posterior odds. For two classes, \[\log\frac{\Pr(1 \mid \mathbf{x})}{\Pr(2 \mid \mathbf{x})} = \log\frac{\pi_1 h_1(r)}{\pi_2 h_2(r)} + \sum_i x_i\,(b_{i2} - b_{i1}).\] The first term is a function of the sum. The second is where the pattern comes in, each item weighted by the difference between the classes’ difficulties.

Levels. If \(b_{i2} - b_{i1} = \delta\) for every item, the second term is \(r\delta\), a function of the sum. Respondents with the same sum have the same posterior, whatever their patterns.

Kinds. If the differences vary across items (the profiles cross), two patterns with the same \(r\) get different posteriors, and the items on which the classes disagree most weigh most. In the widget the content-free class behaves like difficulties of about −1.4 on the forward items and +1.4 on the reversed ones, so a forward item scored 1 pushes toward that class and a reversed item scored 1 pushes away from it.

Screening one respondent at a time

The usual first pass is person-level screens (Meade & Craig, 2012; Curran, 2016): speed, with a cut set by how long reading takes (Huang et al., 2012; Wise & Kong, 2005); sameness, the longest run of one option or the within-person SD (Johnson, 2005); inconsistency between two halves of a scale; oddness, the Mahalanobis distance or person fit; and direct checks, instructed-response and nonsense items (Oppenheimer et al., 2009; Maniaci & Rogge, 2014).

Recall from Person fit: \(l_z\) (mirt’s Zh) standardizes a pattern’s log likelihood at \(\hat\theta\); below 0 is underfit, above 0 overfit. Misfit says the model doesn’t describe the respondent, not why.

A mixture proposes the model that does. But first, do the screens agree?

The content-free class

What does a content-free class do to a scale? That depends on keying.

Recall from From construct map to items: acquiescence is shared by every item, so on an all-forward scale alpha counts it as signal; on a balanced scale it cancels in the sum. Reversed items can form a wording factor.

Recall from Classical test theory and reliability: alpha is a lower bound on reliability only when errors are uncorrelated; correlated errors can push it above reliability.

That widget gave every respondent a little acquiescence. Here a few respondents have all of it.

On the balanced scale every straight line scores exactly 30, the midpoint. Switch to all forward and the class spreads across the whole range, with alpha 1.00 inside it, and the scale’s alpha rises to 0.93. “Agree with everything” on the all-forward scale puts the class near the top (a mean of 45), where it passes for respondents high on the trait. Balanced keying is what makes a content-free class visible (Woods, 2006; Kam & Meyer, 2015; Arias et al., 2020). Weijters, Baumgartner and Schillewaert (2013) count careless responding among the wording factor’s causes; Jin, Chen and Wang (2018), Ulitzsch, Yildirim-Erbasli et al. (2022) and van Laar and Braeken (2022) model the class directly.

NoteGo deeper: a contaminating class’s covariance

Assumptions: two classes with shares \(\pi\) (engaged) and \(1 - \pi\), mean vectors \(\boldsymbol\mu_1, \boldsymbol\mu_0\) and covariance matrices \(\Sigma_1, \Sigma_0\) of the keyed responses.

The mixture’s covariance is the average within-class covariance plus the covariance of the class means: \[\Sigma = \pi\Sigma_1 + (1 - \pi)\Sigma_0 + \pi(1 - \pi)(\boldsymbol\mu_1 - \boldsymbol\mu_0)(\boldsymbol\mu_1 - \boldsymbol\mu_0)^\top.\]

A straight-line class. On a 1–5 scale, a respondent who chooses option \(o\) everywhere has keyed responses \(o\) on forward items and \(6 - o\) on reversed ones: \(3 + (o - 3)\mathbf{v}\), with \(v_i = +1\) for forward and \(-1\) for reversed items. So \(\Sigma_0 = \text{Var}(o)\,\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^\top\), a rank-one term along the keying vector. On an all-forward scale \(\mathbf{v}\) is all ones and lies along the trait’s first factor: covariance is added everywhere and alpha rises. On a balanced scale the term adds covariance within each half and subtracts it across halves, which is a wording factor, and the sum score gets no variance from it (\(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{v} = 0\)), so alpha falls.

An agree-with-everything class has its own mean: on a balanced scale \(\boldsymbol\mu_0 - \boldsymbol\mu_1\) points roughly along \(\mathbf{v}\), and the last term is again close to rank one along the keying vector. This algebra is behind the Su alphas and eigenvalues below.

Response styles as classes

Extreme and midpoint responding are tendencies, not lapses: fairly stable within a respondent and shared by every item (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001). Can a mixture find them?

Recall from Polytomous responses: under the partial credit model, category \(k\) is preferred to \(k - 1\) with log odds \(\theta - b_k\), \(b_k\) being the step difficulty.

In the mixed partial credit model (Rost, 1991) each class has its own steps: squeezed steps send a class to the ends, spread steps keep it in the middle. Both kinds have been reported on questionnaires (Eid & Rauber, 2000; Hernández, Drasgow & González-Romá, 2004; Austin, Deary & Egan, 2006; Wetzel, Carstensen & Böhnke, 2013).

If you’ve done IRTree models, they separate the two by modelling the choice of an end category as its own node (Böckenholt, 2012).

How many classes, and are they real?

Another class always raises the likelihood. BIC is one way to choose \(K\) (Li et al., 2009; Nylund et al., 2007).

Recall from Model fit and out-of-sample prediction: the IMV scores predictions of held-out responses on a portable scale (Domingue et al., 2024).

Held-out responses are the other route: a real class should predict them. Two practical matters follow every fit. Label switching: classes come out in arbitrary order (Stephens, 2000), so name them by profile, not number. Local maxima: a mixture’s likelihood can have several peaks, so fit from several random starts (mirt’s nruns argument; Chalmers, 2012).

Recall from What is a construct?: ordered latent classes close together look very much like a continuum cut into pieces.

The converse holds too. A skewed continuum, or an item model wrong for everyone, can be fitted better by two classes than one, and the classes then describe the model’s shortfall, not kinds of people (Bauer & Curran, 2003; Lubke & Muthén, 2005). So naming a class assumes more than the fit shows, and outside evidence is what backs the name.

When responses can’t tell, times can

On a test with no chance floor, a disengaged respondent and an engaged one who finds every item too hard may both get everything wrong. No mixture of response models can separate identical patterns. What else did the respondent give us? Their time: a disengaged respondent tends to answer quickly and an engaged low scorer slowly (Wang & Xu, 2015; Ulitzsch, Pohl et al., 2022). The simplest version gives each class its own distribution of mean log time per item. If you’ve done Response time and the speed-accuracy tradeoff, it modelled log time item by item; the full joint model is beyond this lesson.

With real data

Antonyms on three platforms

Our main example, kay_2025_antonyms, is a data-quality probe (Kay, 2025; survey and data on OSF, CC BY 4.0): 1,200 respondents from Connect, Prolific and MTurk rated 29 pairs of statements, mostly opposites such as “I am an extravert” and “I am an introvert” (wording from the survey), from −3 to +3, alongside four instructed-response items. # Two classes: engaged respondents follow the Rasch model (theta ~ normal(0, s^2));
# disengaged respondents answer every item correctly with one common probability g.
# With use_time = TRUE each class also has its own normal distribution of mean log
# time. EM on a grid of abilities, extending fit_mixture() from the lesson on
# guessing and priors. A few seconds.
fit_rt_mixture <- function(X, logt, use_time, Q = 41, maxit = 2000, tol = 1e-7) {
  pl <- function(x) pmin(pmax(plogis(x), 1e-10), 1 - 1e-10)
  obs <- !is.na(X); X0 <- ifelse(obs, X, 0)
  z <- seq(-5, 5, length.out = Q); wz <- dnorm(z) / sum(dnorm(z))
  b <- -qlogis(pmin(pmax(colMeans(X, na.rm = TRUE), 0.01), 0.99)); s <- 1; pi <- 0.8; g <- 0.05
  mu <- c(mean(logt), mean(logt) - 0.5); sdv <- c(sd(logt), sd(logt)); old <- -Inf
  for (it in 1:maxit) {
    P <- pl(outer(s * z, b, "-"))
    a <- X0 %*% t(log(P)) + (obs * (1 - X0)) %*% t(log(1 - P)) + rep(log(wz), each = nrow(X))
    mx <- apply(a, 1, max); llR <- mx + log(rowSums(exp(a - mx))); llE <- llR
    llD <- rowSums(X0) * log(g) + rowSums(obs * (1 - X0)) * log(1 - g)
    if (use_time) {
      llE <- llE + dnorm(logt, mu[1], sdv[1], log = TRUE)
      llD <- llD + dnorm(logt, mu[2], sdv[2], log = TRUE)
    }
    both <- cbind(log(pi) + llE, log(1 - pi) + llD)
    mm <- apply(both, 1, max); lli <- mm + log(rowSums(exp(both - mm))); ll <- sum(lli)
    pE <- exp(both[, 1] - lli); pD <- 1 - pE
    Wt <- exp(a - llR) * pE                     # posterior over the grid, engaged class
    nq <- t(Wt) %*% obs; r <- t(Wt) %*% X0
    pi <- mean(pE); g <- sum(pD * rowSums(X0)) / sum(pD * rowSums(obs))
    for (k in 1:5) {
      P <- pl(outer(s * z, b, "-"))
      b <- b + pmax(pmin(colSums(nq * P - r) / colSums(nq * P * (1 - P)), 1), -1)
    }
    s <- exp(optimize(function(ls) { P <- pl(outer(exp(ls) * z, b, "-"))
      -sum(r * log(P) + (nq - r) * log(1 - P)) }, c(-2, 1.5))$minimum)
    if (use_time) {
      mu  <- c(sum(pE * logt) / sum(pE), sum(pD * logt) / sum(pD))
      sdv <- sqrt(c(sum(pE * (logt - mu[1])^2) / sum(pE), sum(pD * (logt - mu[2])^2) / sum(pD)))
    }
    if (abs(ll - old) < tol) break
    old <- ll
  }
  list(pi = pi, g = g, p_disengaged = pD, seconds = exp(mu), loglik = ll)
}

The assumptions are on the table: the classes are kinds of respondent, not a continuum the model gets wrong; the cuts and \(K\) were fixed before the substantive analysis; outside evidence agrees. The two kinds of evidence answer different questions. Held-out responses say whether a class predicts: on the Kay data two classes gained 0.17 over the Rasch model, but the 2PL gained 0.16 on its own and the mixture only 0.0088 more, so comparing the mixture with its rivals, not only with one class, is what tells you how much the class adds. The platform, the nonsense items and the times say what the class is. Where a score feeds a decision about a person, as in a campus screening, a high posterior is a probability about how someone answered, and a class is a hypothesis about that, not a verdict on who they are.

Problems

  1. Derivation. Write the posterior class probability under a two-class mixture Rasch model, and show that beyond the sum it depends on the pattern only through \(\sum_i x_i(b_{i2} - b_{i1})\). Then show that on a balanced \(2m\)-item 1–5 scale a same-option respondent always scores the midpoint. What happens on an all-forward scale, and when every raw response is raised by an acquiescence \(\delta\) (ignoring the ceiling)?
  2. Real data with a twist: strategies. balance_mokken holds 25 balance-scale tasks answered by 484 children (van Maanen, Been & Sijtsma, 1989; read it from the CSV link on its landing page). Fit mixture Rasch models with two and three classes. Do the classes’ profiles cross, and do they match the rules Siegler (1976) proposed (Jansen & van der Maas, 1997)? What would make a class a strategy rather than a level?
  3. Judgment. In depression_anxiety_stress, a voluntary online DASS (Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995), some respondents answered “applied to me very much” to all 42 items. Is that a straight line, or severe distress? Use their times, the fake words in the checklist that followed, and the design (items were shown in a random order). What can’t the data tell, and what would you do in a campus screening where a score leads to a follow-up call?
  4. Design. Plan a 20-item survey on which both screens and a mixture can find inattentive respondents: keying, antonym pairs, instructed items, nonsense items, timing. Say what each buys, and set the cuts before collecting data. Then a colleague drops everyone in the Kay content-free class before correlating extraversion with political orientation (cov_poli_cont). What changes, and what should happen to respondents with posteriors near 0.5 (Credé, 2010)?
  5. Real data: latent DIF. Which Kay items function differently between the two classes, and how closely does the class line up with the platform (Cohen & Bolt, 2005; if you’ve done Differential item functioning, compare)? When is a class an observed group under another name? Then refit on the three neuroticism pairs (mood, upst, rlxd): is the content-free class a property of the respondent or of the scale?
  6. Challenge (open). Fit a mixed graded or partial credit model to the full seven-point Kay ratings and look for style classes beside the content-free one. Does the gain survive against a skewed continuum, simulated to match? I don’t know of a general test that tells a real class from a non-normal continuum.

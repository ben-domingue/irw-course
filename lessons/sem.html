What this is for

Does grit go with feeling good? A regression of one sum score on another answers a smaller question than it seems to: it relates two error-laden measures, and error shrinks and shuffles the coefficients. Factor analysis II modelled each construct as a factor behind its items. A structural equation model (SEM) keeps those factors and adds regressions between them, so the relations are estimated between the constructs rather than between their scores (Jöreskog, 1970; Bollen, 1989). That buys a correction for measurement error, and it costs assumptions: a measurement model that must be right before the paths mean anything, and paths that look causal whether or not they are. We use the Grit-O and the PANAS, answered by the same children and adolescents in the Florida Twin Project.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • say what the measurement and structural parts of an SEM are, and write both in lavaan syntax;
  • explain how measurement error biases regressions on sum scores, including with two correlated predictors, and why a latent regression doesn’t carry that bias when its model holds;
  • check by counting whether a structural model can be identified, and say which part of the model a fit index describes;
  • fit and inspect a measurement model before the structural one, and compare latent paths with sum-score regressions on real data;
  • say why a well-fitting path model is not evidence for its causal direction, and write down an equivalent model.

Core ideas

A measurement model and a structural model

An SEM has two parts. The measurement model is a confirmatory factor model: each factor \(\eta\) is measured by its items, \(x_i = \nu_i + \lambda_i \eta + \varepsilon_i\). The structural model relates the factors to each other with regressions, \[\eta_{\text{outcome}} = \beta_1 \eta_1 + \beta_2 \eta_2 + \zeta,\] where \(\zeta\) is the part of the outcome the predictors leave unexplained. Path analysis, regressions among observed variables drawn as arrows, goes back to Wright (1934); Jöreskog (1970) joined it to the factor model and fitted both by maximum likelihood.

Our model has four factors: two facets of grit (perseverance of effort, consistency of interest) and two kinds of affect (positive, negative). The question is how the grit facets relate to affect, with each construct measured by its items.

Why a latent path differs from a regression on sum scores

Recall from Classical test theory and reliability, problem 2: a correlation between two error-laden scores is attenuated, \(\text{cor}(X, Y) = \text{cor}(T_X, T_Y)\sqrt{\rho_{XX'}\,\rho_{YY'}}\) (Spearman, 1904). If you’ve done Gathering validity evidence, you corrected validity coefficients for it by dividing by that square root.

With one predictor, the standardized regression slope is the correlation, so it shrinks by the same factor. Error in the outcome and error in the predictor act differently on the unstandardized slope, though: error in \(Y\) adds noise around the line without tilting it, while error in \(X\) flattens it by the factor \(\rho_{XX'}\) (problem 1).

With two correlated predictors the damage is no longer a simple shrinkage. Controlling for a predictor measured with error controls for it only in part, so what is left of it leaks into the other predictor’s coefficient (Cole & Preacher, 2014; Westfall & Yarkoni, 2016). An SEM avoids both problems when its model holds: the paths run between the factors, which carry no measurement error, and the correction for attenuation is built into the model rather than applied by hand. A regression on sum scores rests on different assumptions: that the scores are reliable enough for the shrinkage and the leak to be small, or that the score itself, not the construct, is what will be used, as with a cut score.

Specifying the model in lavaan

Recall from Factor analysis II: in lavaan (Rosseel, 2012), =~ reads “is measured by”, and each factor’s scale is set by fixing one loading or its variance. The degrees of freedom are the variances and covariances the data supply minus the parameters the model spends.

Two more operators finish the language: ~ reads “is regressed on”, and ~~ declares a variance or covariance. The structural part of our model is two lines added under the four =~ lines:

model <- "per =~ g1 + g4 + g6 + g9 + g10 + g12
          con =~ g2 + g3 + g5 + g7 + g8 + g11
          pa  =~ p1 + p3 + p5 + p9 + p10 + p12 + p14 + p16 + p17 + p19
          na  =~ p2 + p4 + p6 + p7 + p8 + p11 + p13 + p15 + p18 + p20
          pa ~ per + con
          na ~ per + con"
fit <- sem(model, data = d, estimator = "MLR")

sem() lets the two predictors correlate and, by default, lets the two outcomes’ residuals \(\zeta\) correlate too.

Can the structural part be identified? The measurement model already estimates the six correlations among four factors. The structural model re-expresses them with the predictors’ correlation, the paths and any residual covariance. With more than six such parameters, some can’t be recovered from the data. Counting is necessary but not sufficient for identification (Bollen, 1989).

The estimator matters as it did in CFA. Maximum likelihood assumes multivariate normal items. MLR keeps the ML estimates and makes the standard errors and the test statistic robust to non-normality (Yuan & Bentler, 2000); WLSMV treats items as ordered categories and suits items with few or skewed categories (Rhemtulla, Brosseau-Liard & Savalei, 2012).

Fit, and fit of which part

A global fit index compares the whole implied covariance matrix with the data. In an SEM most of that matrix is the measurement model: in ours, 32 items supply 528 variances and covariances, and the structural claims concern at most six factor correlations. Misfit among the items can swamp the structure, and a structural part with few degrees of freedom of its own can be wrong without the global indices showing it. Anderson and Gerbing (1988) proposed two steps: fit the measurement model alone, with every factor correlated, and settle it; then fit the structural model and test its constraints against the measurement model. The cutoffs of Hu and Bentler (1999) come from simulations of factor models, and a structural model inherits whatever misfit its measurement model carries.

Fitting everything in one step gives the same estimates. In the case that the measurement model is well established in similar samples, one step may be sufficient for some purposes; what it can’t do is say which part a misfit belongs to.

Recall from Factor analysis II: fit indices say how far off a model is, and the residuals and modification indices show where. Zhang et al. (2026) add prediction on held-out respondents, measured by the IMV, as a complement to fit for CFA with binary items.

Fitting and inspecting the measurement model first says how well the items measure each factor before any structural path is read. A path between factors that the items don’t measure well is an estimate of something, but not of the relation the diagram names, and the structural fit indices won’t say so.

Factors or composites? Everything here models a construct as a common factor, the source of what its items share. Composite-based SEM (partial least squares, consistent PLS, generalized structured component analysis) models a construct as a weighted sum of its items instead. It is a different model with different assumptions, closer to a construct that is defined by its indicators, as an index of socioeconomic status is. In R it is fitted by cSEM (Rademaker & Schuberth, 2020; on the model, Schuberth, Henseler & Dijkstra, 2018), and in jamovi by the Composite-SEM module, a small project without a licence file as of September 2026. For point-and-click factor-based SEM, jamovi’s SEMLj module builds lavaan models from menus.

What a path can’t tell you

An arrow in a path diagram is a regression coefficient. Whether it is a causal effect depends on assumptions the fit doesn’t test: no omitted common causes, the right direction, and a design that supports both (Bollen & Pearl, 2013). And for most models there are equivalent models, with different arrows, that imply exactly the same covariance matrix and so fit identically on any data (MacCallum, Wegener, Uchino & Fabrigar, 1993). Whether the constructs themselves cause their indicators is a further question, which Validity as causation takes up.

Simulate

The code simulates a latent predictor and a latent outcome with a standardized path of 0.5, each measured by five items, at three levels of item quality. For each it compares the correlation of the sum scores (the standardized slope of a one-predictor regression), Spearman’s prediction of it, and the path from sem(). The first run takes 20–30 seconds while lavaan loads.

With the seed as written, the sum-score slope falls from 0.45 to 0.24 as the loadings drop from 0.8 to 0.4, within 0.01 of Spearman’s prediction each time, while the SEM path stays between 0.50 and 0.56. The SEM estimate is unbiased, not free: with loadings of 0.4 it moves more from sample to sample. Change the seed, or set np <- 200, to see how much; with np <- 5000 (locally) it settles near 0.5.

Children and adolescents

Recall from Measurement invariance: comparing factor correlations or regressions across groups needs metric invariance (equal loadings), and comparing means needs scalar invariance.

Do the paths hold for children aged 9–13 and adolescents aged 14–18? A multigroup SEM fits the model in both groups at once and adds constraints: first on the loadings, then on the paths.

Problems

  1. Attenuation of a slope. Let \(X = T_X + E_X\) and \(Y = \beta T_X + \zeta + E_Y\), with each error uncorrelated with everything else. Show that the population slope of \(Y\) on \(X\) is \(\beta \rho_{XX'}\) and that \(E_Y\) doesn’t bias it. Then find the standardized slope and compare it with Spearman’s formula.
  2. Factor scores, with a twist. Refit the sum-score regressions with factor scores from the measurement model (lavPredict(m1)) in place of the sum scores. Are the paths closer to the latent ones or to the sum-score ones? Why doesn’t using factor scores remove the bias, and which kind of factor score for the predictors and which for the outcome would (Skrondal & Laake, 2001)?
  3. Twins. The participants come in pairs. What does clustering do to standard errors, and why did it do so little here? Compute the correlation of twins’ sum scores within families. What would a model that used the pairing as information, rather than a nuisance, look like?
  4. Design. Draw an SEM for a question you care about, with at least two latent predictors and one latent outcome. Count its structural degrees of freedom. Which path would you most like to read causally, and what design would let you?
  5. Mend the measurement model. Free the error covariance between PANAS items 7 and 20 (and, if you like, 8 and 11). How much does fit improve, and do the structural paths change? Then drop items 3 and 11 from consistency of interest. Which change matters more for the paths, and which would you defend in a paper?
  6. Challenge (open). Write down a model equivalent to ours in which affect predicts grit, and one in which a third factor causes both. What evidence (waves, twins, an intervention) could separate them? The PANAS was asked in all three waves, grit in one; I don’t know how far these data can go.

What this is for

In 2011, Austrian fourth graders sitting TIMSS each took one of 14 mathematics booklets. Students given booklet 13 averaged 15.0 of its 26 items; those given booklet 11 averaged 11.6. Is the booklet-13 group better at mathematics, or is booklet 13 easier? The booklets were handed out at random, so it is the booklet. The Rasch model said that \(\hat\theta\) earns its keep when respondents take different items, and this lesson collects. Putting scores from different forms on one scale is linking; its strictest form, equating, makes them interchangeable. Whether either is possible is settled mostly by the design. After that, everything rests on an assumption about the items the forms share, which the TIMSS booklets let us check: it is off by a small amount per link, and fourteen links add it up.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • say what equating requires (symmetry, equity, population invariance) and which data-collection designs make it possible;
  • equate two forms by mean, linear and equipercentile equating, and say when each is a reasonable choice;
  • put two IRT calibrations on one scale with mean-mean, mean-sigma, Haebara or Stocking–Lord linking, or with one concurrent fit, and convert raw scores by IRT true-score equating;
  • say what linking assumes about the common items, and check it in data (position effects, items that behave differently across groups);
  • tell equating from the looser kinds of linking: vertical scaling and concordance.

Core ideas

Two scenarios, one problem

Recall from Where CTT breaks: the sum score depends on which items were taken. The same 664 students averaged 9.6 on an easy half of a test and 5.7 on a hard half, and no single constant converted one half’s score into the other’s.

A testing program writes a new form each year to the same blueprint and needs a score of 30 this year to mean what 30 meant last year. That is equating: afterwards, which form a respondent took shouldn’t matter. A grade 3 test and a grade 4 test that share some items are built to different specifications; their scores can go on one scale, to talk about growth, but can’t be made interchangeable. That is vertical scaling, one kind of the broader family called linking (Linn, 1993; Dorans, Pommerich & Holland, 2007; Kolen & Brennan, 2014). What growth on such a scale means is a question for Scale properties. Looser still, a concordance pairs scores with the same percentile rank on tests built by different programs, such as the SAT and the ACT, without claiming the scores are interchangeable (Dorans, 2004). This lesson is about equating, and about linking forms through the items they share.

What equating asks for

What would make a converted score as good as the original? The literature asks for three properties (Kolen & Brennan, 2014, ch. 1; Dorans & Holland, 2000):

  • Symmetry. Converting form X to form Y and back again returns the score we started with.
  • Equity. A respondent at any level of the construct should be indifferent to which form they get (Lord, 1980).
  • Population invariance. The conversion shouldn’t depend on the group it was computed in; Dorans and Holland read one that does as a sign that the tests shouldn’t be equated.

Why not regression? To predict a respondent’s score on Y from their score on X, regression is the right tool. But predictions shrink toward the mean, so predicting Y from X doesn’t invert predicting X from Y, and symmetry fails.

The two regression lines disagree, each pulling toward the mean from its own side. Linear equating sits between them, matching means and SDs and ignoring the correlation. Only at a correlation of 1 do the three lines meet.

Design comes first. Equating is impossible unless the data were collected so that a solution exists. Three designs do that (Kolen & Brennan, 2014, ch. 1):

  • Random groups. Forms are handed out at random, so any difference in scores belongs to the forms. No common items are needed.
  • Single group, counterbalanced. Everyone takes both forms, half in each order, so that order effects cancel.
  • Common-item nonequivalent groups (NEAT: nonequivalent groups with an anchor test). The groups may differ (this year’s cohort and last year’s), and common items, the anchor, carry the link, so everything depends on the anchor behaving the same way in both groups.

TIMSS combines two of these: booklets are spiralled through classrooms (random groups), and each booklet also shares a block of items with the next (common items).

Observed-score equating

With random groups, the two score distributions estimate how one population would score on each form, so matching the distributions matches the forms.

  • Mean equating adds a constant: \(y = x - \mu_X + \mu_Y\). Reasonable when the forms differ only in overall difficulty.
  • Linear equating matches means and SDs: \(y = \mu_Y + \frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x - \mu_X)\), the black line in the widget above. Reasonable when the forms differ in spread too and the distributions have similar shapes.
  • Equipercentile equating sends a score on X to the score on Y with the same percentile rank (Kolen & Brennan, 2014, ch. 2). It is the one to reach for when the shapes differ, for instance when one form has a ceiling. Small samples give bumpy distributions, so they are usually smoothed first, for instance by log-linear models that keep their first few moments (Holland & Thayer, 2000).

The equate package (Albano, 2016) does all three.

IRT linking: through common items, or in one fit

Recall from The Rasch model: only \(\theta - b\) is identified, so the scale has no origin, and mirt sets one by fixing the mean ability at 0. From the 1PL to the 4PL added the unit: the 2PL sees only \(a(\theta - b)\), so software fixes the SD of ability at 1.

Calibrate form X in one group and form Y in another, and each fit sets its origin and unit by its own group. The same item then gets parameters in the two fits related by a linear map: with \(\theta_Y = A\,\theta_X + B\), \[b_Y = A\,b_X + B, \qquad a_Y = a_X / A,\] which leaves every \(a(\theta - b)\), and so every probability, unchanged. Under the Rasch model \(A = 1\) and only the shift \(B\) is needed. The common items give two sets of estimates of the same parameters, from which to find \(A\) and \(B\):

  • Mean-mean (Loyd & Hoover, 1980): \(A = \bar a_X / \bar a_Y\), then \(B = \bar b_Y - A\,\bar b_X\).
  • Mean-sigma (Marco, 1977): \(A = s(b_Y)/s(b_X)\), then \(B\) as before.
  • Haebara (1980): choose \(A\) and \(B\) to bring each common item’s transformed curve close to its curve in the other calibration, over a grid of \(\theta\).
  • Stocking–Lord (Stocking & Lord, 1983): the same for the sum of the curves, the common items’ test characteristic curve (TCC).

Recall from What is measurement?: an interval scale allows exactly the linear relabellings \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\), and whether a scale has an equal unit changes group comparisons.

What does each approach assume about the scale? Equipercentile equating allows any order-preserving map: it treats sum scores as ordinal. IRT linking allows only linear maps, the interval scale’s family: it takes \(\theta\) to have an equal unit that the two fits labelled differently. So we link on \(\theta\) and convert to raw scores afterwards if needed. In IRT true-score equating (Lord, 1980), a raw score \(x\) on form X is sent to the \(\theta\) at which X’s TCC equals \(x\), and then to Y’s TCC at that \(\theta\).

The small panels show nine of the items: each one’s second-calibration curve before (light) and after (blue) your \(A\) and \(B\), and its first-calibration curve (dashed), the target. Move one slider and every item moves at once: a link transforms the whole scale, not one item. Close the shaded gap and you have done Stocking–Lord by eye. With no estimation error all three methods recover 1.2 and 0.5. With error they part: at the default SD of 0.15, Stocking–Lord gives 1.23 and 0.55, mean-sigma 1.28 and 0.57. Mean-sigma rests on the difficulties’ spread, which estimation error inflates.

Or skip the linking. IRT handles responses missing by design, so we can stack every form’s data, leave each respondent’s unseen items empty and fit once: concurrent calibration. In the TIMSS booklets most pairs of items are never answered by the same student (Item response data and the IRW counted them), and one fit copes. In simulations it generally gave smaller errors than separate calibration and linking, though not always (Kim & Cohen, 1998; Hanson & Béguin, 2002). Both routes assume a common item’s parameters are the same in every group; a concurrent fit builds that in, while separate calibrations let you compare. Concurrent calibration is a reasonable choice when the groups come from one population or differ only in their ability distributions; separate calibration, when you want to see how the common items behaved.

NoteGo deeper: how the characteristic-curve methods weight items

Assumptions: two calibrations of the same \(n\) common items under the 2PL, with estimates \((\hat a_{iX}, \hat b_{iX})\) and \((\hat a_{iY}, \hat b_{iY})\); the true parameters satisfy \(b_{iY} = A b_{iX} + B\) and \(a_{iY} = a_{iX}/A\); a weight function \(w(\theta)\) on Y’s scale over a grid \(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_Q\) (for instance a normal density).

Moment methods. Mean-sigma sets \(A = s(\hat b_Y)/s(\hat b_X)\). Every item counts equally, however well it is estimated. An item with a slope near zero has a difficulty that is a ratio with a small denominator (\(b = -d/a\) in mirt), so its \(\hat b\) can be far out and dominate both SDs. Mean-mean takes \(A\) from the slopes, which avoids that particular problem, but it still weights every item equally.

Characteristic-curve methods. Stocking–Lord minimizes \[F(A, B) = \sum_{q=1}^{Q} w(\theta_q)\, D_q^2, \qquad D_q = \sum_i P_i(\theta_q;\, \hat a_{iY}, \hat b_{iY}) - \sum_i P_i\big(\theta_q;\, \hat a_{iX}/A,\, A \hat b_{iX} + B\big).\] Write \(P^*_{iq}\) for the transformed curve, \(\text{logit}^{-1}\big((\hat a_{iX}/A)(\theta_q - A\hat b_{iX} - B)\big)\). Since \(\partial P^*_{iq}/\partial B = -(\hat a_{iX}/A)\, P^*_{iq}(1 - P^*_{iq})\), \[\frac{\partial F}{\partial B} = 2\sum_q w(\theta_q)\, D_q \sum_i \frac{\hat a_{iX}}{A}\, P^*_{iq}(1 - P^*_{iq}),\] and \(\partial F/\partial A\) has the same form, with a further factor \((\theta_q - B)/A\) inside the sum over items. An item enters in proportion to \(a\,P(1-P)\), its information (Information, precision, and short forms), at the \(\theta\) values that carry weight. A flat item, or one far from the respondents, barely moves the answer, however extreme its \(\hat b\). Haebara’s criterion squares each item’s difference before summing over items, so each item’s curve must match on its own; Stocking–Lord lets errors in different items cancel.

What follows. The characteristic-curve methods match what the link is for, expected scores where the respondents are, and are less sensitive to poorly estimated items. Moment methods have closed forms and are a reasonable first look, and a large gap between the two families is itself a sign that some common items need a look. The same idea returns when choosing anchor items in Differential item functioning and when adding items to a bank in Item banks and adaptive testing.

How linking breaks

Every method above assumes that a common item is the same item in both places. What can make it not so? Its position or context: an item late in a long test, or next to different items, can be harder there (Leary & Dorans, 1985; Debeer & Janssen, 2013). Drift: an item reused over years can become easier as it is taught to or exposed. Translation: an item in another language is, strictly, another item. Michaelides (2010) reviews what such items do to linking. Each is a form of differential item functioning, an item working differently in two groups matched on the construct (Differential item functioning). And links chain: link C to B and B to A, and each link’s error, and any bias it carries, is passed along. Haberman (2009) develops a regression that links many separately calibrated forms at once rather than pair by pair.

md`After 14 links form 1 is **${rw_path[14].position.toFixed(2)}** logits from where it started. The bias alone contributes 14 × ${rw_bias.toFixed(2)} = ${(14 * rw_bias).toFixed(2)}; the random errors add up to an SD of ${rw_noise.toFixed(2)} × √14 = ${(rw_noise * Math.sqrt(14)).toFixed(2)}.`

With no bias the path wanders and the gap is modest (0.15 with the first seed). Random errors grow with the square root of the number of links, so they partly cancel. A bias doesn’t: set it to 0.05 and the path climbs, whatever the seed. A ring is useful because it tells us where we should end up.

A link is an assumption about the common items, and the design decides whether you can check it.

Simulate

The code below gives each of 600 respondents 15 of a bank of 50 Rasch items, in three designs from EDUC 252’s problem set 6: a chain of 8 forms, each overlapping the next by 10 items; 5 forms linked by 5 items, looping back to the first; and 15 random items each. A fourth design repeats the chain but makes an item 0.3 logits harder in the last third of a form. Each design is fit once, concurrently. The first run takes 20–30 seconds while R and mirt load, and the fits a little longer.

With the seed as written, the three designs recover the bank about equally well (correlations 0.96 to 0.97; root mean squared errors 0.16 to 0.19). With the position effect the correlation drops to 0.90, and the errors run along the chain, from 0.42 too easy for the first five items to 0.65 too hard for the last five: the fit has no term for position, so the common items carry it from form to form. At np <- 150 every correlation drops (0.81 to 0.87). At 3,000, in your own R session, the three designs reach 0.99, and the position effect still spreads the errors from −0.33 to 0.39: more data don’t remove a bias.

With real data

TIMSS 2011: random groups and a ring of booklets

Our main example is cdm_timss11, 4,668 Austrian fourth graders’ mathematics responses in TIMSS 2011, released with the CDM package by George and Robitzsch (2014). Its job is to show both kinds of design on one table and to test the assumption linking rests on; Item response data and the IRW used it for missingness by design. TIMSS 2011 put its 14 mathematics blocks into 14 booklets, each block in two: M02 is the second mathematics block of booklet 1 and the first of booklet 2, and booklet 14 carries M01 round to booklet 1 (Mullis, Martin, Ruddock, O’Sullivan & Preuschoff, 2009, Exhibit 11). Booklets were distributed so that their groups are approximately equivalent (the same source, ch. 4). A response is 1 when correct; parts of one question (IDs ending A, B, C) are treated as separate items. Download the code to run every chunk yourself.

The ring. The design also lets us check the common items. Calibrate each booklet on its own (Rasch), so each fit centres its own group at 0. With equivalent groups those origins should agree, and mean-mean linking from each booklet to the next should find shifts near zero. Chain the links from booklet 1 through 14 and back to 1.

# Calibrate each booklet on its own (Rasch; mirt centres each group's abilities at 0),
# then link booklet k+1 to booklet k by mean-mean linking through their common block:
# the shift is the mean difficulty of the common items in k minus that in k+1.
calib <- lapply(1:14, function(k) {
  m <- mirt(long2wide(tm[tm$booklet == k, ]), 1, itemtype = "Rasch", verbose = FALSE)
  d <- coef(m, simplify = TRUE)$items[, "d"]
  -d                                               # Rasch: b = -d
})
shift <- sapply(1:14, function(k) {
  k2 <- k %% 14 + 1
  common <- intersect(names(calib[[k]]), names(calib[[k2]]))
  mean(calib[[k]][common]) - mean(calib[[k2]][common])
})

The ring misses by 1.08 logits. Why? Each link’s common block is the second mathematics block of one booklet and the first of the next, so if an item is harder when it comes second, every link picks up the same bias. A Rasch model with a term for the second block, fit as a mixed logistic regression (Bates, Mächler, Bolker & Walker, 2015), estimates it directly:

An item is 0.077 logits harder in the second block (SE 0.014), and 14 × 0.077 = 1.08: the position effect accounts for the gap. Most of it is at the end. An item costs 0.12 logits more in position 4, the last block of the test, than in position 1 (SE 0.034); between positions 2 and 3, either side of the break, the difference is 0.035, within its error. Per item the effect is small; fourteen links make it large.

So which would I use for booklets like these, concurrent calibration or separate calibration and linking? Both assume a common item has one set of parameters wherever it appears. The concurrent fit builds that in, giving each item one difficulty averaged over its two positions; separate calibrations show whether the common items agree. I’d report from the concurrent fit, which uses all the data at once, and run separate calibrations alongside it, because only they could have shown me this position effect.

We fit a 2PL in each country and link the other three to Austria by all four methods. Each fit sets its country’s mean at 0 and SD at 1, so \(B\) is the country’s mean in Austrian SD units and \(A\) its SD relative to Austria’s.

Code 
# A separate 2PL for each country; each fit sets its own country's mean to 0 and SD to 1.
pfit <- lapply(c(AUT = "AUT", DEU = "DEU", FRA = "FRA", NLD = "NLD"),
               function(k) mirt(pw[country == k, ], 1, itemtype = "2PL", verbose = FALSE))
ab <- lapply(pfit, function(m) coef(m, IRTpars = TRUE, simplify = TRUE)$items[, c("a", "b")])
# Put form X (a country) on the scale of form Y (Austria): theta_Y = A theta_X + B,
# so b -> A b + B and a -> a / A.
grid_th <- seq(-4, 4, length.out = 81); w_th <- dnorm(grid_th)   # quadrature on Austria's scale
icc <- function(a, b) plogis(outer(grid_th, b, "-") * rep(a, each = length(grid_th)))
link <- function(X, Y, method) {
  aX <- X[, "a"]; bX <- X[, "b"]; aY <- Y[, "a"]; bY <- Y[, "b"]
  if (method == "mean-mean")  { A <- mean(aX) / mean(aY); return(c(A = A, B = mean(bY) - A * mean(bX))) }
  if (method == "mean-sigma") { A <- sd(bY) / sd(bX);     return(c(A = A, B = mean(bY) - A * mean(bX))) }
  PY <- icc(aY, bY)
  loss <- function(p) {
    PX <- icc(aX / p[1], p[1] * bX + p[2])
    if (method == "Haebara") sum(w_th * rowSums((PY - PX)^2))       # item by item
    else sum(w_th * (rowSums(PY) - rowSums(PX))^2)                   # Stocking-Lord: the sums
  }
  setNames(optim(c(1, 0), loss)$par, c("A", "B"))
}
methods <- c("mean-mean", "mean-sigma", "Haebara", "Stocking-Lord")
# B is each country's mean in Austrian SD units; A is its SD relative to Austria's.

A <- sapply(c("DEU", "FRA", "NLD"), function(k) sapply(methods, function(m) link(ab[[k]], ab$AUT, m)["A"]))

Problems

  1. Derivation. Show that under the 2PL, with \(\theta_Y = A\theta_X + B\), the maps \(b_Y = Ab_X + B\) and \(a_Y = a_X/A\) leave every probability unchanged, and derive the mean-mean constants. Then show that predicting Y from X by regression, and X back from that by the other regression, returns the starting score only if the correlation is 1.
  2. A devious split (from EDUC 252). Take TIMSS booklets 11 and 12, which share a block, and keep only booklet 12’s top 70% by sum score. Link 12 to 11 through the common block (mean-mean, Rasch), and equate the sum scores by equipercentile equating as if the groups were random. What happens to each? Which notices the selection?
  3. Judgment. Make the best case for reporting TIMSS results from separate calibrations rather than the concurrent fit. What would you do about the 1.08-logit gap?
  4. Design (EDUC 252, problem set 6). You can test 1,500 respondents, 15 items each, from a 50-item bank. Using the simulation, compare the three designs, then shrink the loop’s links to two items, or one. When does the design start to matter?
  5. Real data. In the PIRLS data, which would you trust more, Germany’s link to Austria or France’s? What would you check first?
  6. Challenge (open). A testing program moves a block of common items from the start of this year’s form to the end of next year’s. Design a study that could tell a position effect from a change in the population. What would you randomize, and what would it cost? I don’t know of a design that settles this cheaply.

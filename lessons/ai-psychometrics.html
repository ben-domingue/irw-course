What this is for

Language models now score essays, write items, predict item difficulty, and answer questionnaires in place of people. Each is a claim about measurement that the tools of this course can check. The lesson starts from the question What is an item? handed on: that lesson explained difficulty with features that people coded, so can an item’s text alone predict it? My answer depends on the yardstick. I judge a difficulty prediction by how many respondents it is worth, and on the general-knowledge items below the best text features are worth about three.

Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • compute exact agreement, Cohen’s kappa and quadratic weighted kappa from two raters’ scores, and say what an automated scorer’s agreement with human raters does and doesn’t show;
  • add text features to the LLTM and report how much of the variation in difficulty they explain;
  • convert the error of a difficulty prediction into the number of respondents it is worth;
  • say why more simulated respondents don’t fix a language model’s own view of the items;
  • check machine-written items as you would any others: difficulty, distractors, keying, and whether each item goes with the construct it was written for.

Core ideas

Two intersections

AI and psychometrics meet in two directions. One uses AI to measure people. The other uses psychometrics to measure AI: a benchmark is a set of items and a model is a respondent, and Martínez-Plumed, Prudêncio, Martínez-Usó and Hernández-Orallo (2019) fit item response models to machine-learning classifiers, one item per test instance. The IRW holds no table of models answering questions yet, so that direction gets problem 6; the rest of the lesson is about the first.

Automated scoring is an agreement problem

An essay-scoring engine is trained to reproduce the scores human raters give; e-rater is a long-running example (Attali & Burstein, 2006). How do we know it works? The first evidence is agreement with human raters, read from the table that crosses the engine’s scores with a human’s. Three statistics summarize that table:

  • exact agreement, the share of responses on the diagonal;
  • Cohen’s kappa, exact agreement corrected for the agreement two raters would reach by chance with the same margins, \(\kappa = (p_o - p_e)/(1 - p_e)\);
  • quadratic weighted kappa (QWK; Cohen, 1968), which charges each disagreement by its squared distance: with weights \(w_{ik} = (i - k)^2\), observed counts \(O_{ik}\) and the counts \(E_{ik}\) expected from the margins, \[\text{QWK} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,k} w_{ik} O_{ik}}{\sum_{i,k} w_{ik} E_{ik}}.\]

Agreement with what? The baseline is the agreement of two human raters scoring the same responses. Williamson, Xi and Breyer (2012) make human–machine agreement one strand of the evidence for an engine, beside its fit to the purpose, its relations with independent measures, its generalizability across tasks and forms, and its consequences for subgroups.

As the widget loads, the engine agrees with human 1 about as well as human 2 does (QWK 0.70 against 0.65; the gap is sampling noise). Shift it up half a point: exact agreement falls from 0.54 to 0.47, QWK only to 0.65. Compress its scale to 0.6 and QWK drops to 0.55. Then cut its noise to 0.3 with the scale at 0.7: it matches the human baseline (0.66) while still hugging the middle. The same QWK can come from quite different engines.

QWK depends on the margins of the table and the length of the scale (Lewis & Casabianca, 2026). The proportional reduction in mean squared error (PRMSE) judges an engine against the human raters’ true scores, allowing for their unreliability, and can reach a different conclusion from QWK on the same data (McCaffrey, Casabianca & Johnson, 2025). And agreement says nothing about what the engine responds to: in 240,000 admission essays, content and style correlated more strongly with household income than SAT scores did (Alvero et al., 2021). An engine could agree with human raters and carry that signal too; Williamson et al.’s subgroup evidence is how you would find out.

If you’ve done Rater models: an engine is one more rater, with a severity of its own. Casabianca, Donoghue, Shin, Chao and Choi (2023) found that two human ratings connected raters better than one human rating plus an engine’s, and that engine scores alone did not connect raters adequately.

NoteGo deeper: QWK is a correlation that charges for differences in mean and spread

Assumptions: two raters score the same \(n\) responses on an integer scale; \(p_{ik}\) is the observed share with scores \((i, k)\); the expected shares are the products of the margins, \(p_{i\cdot}\,p_{\cdot k}\); weights are \((i - k)^2\); means, variances and the covariance are computed with divisor \(n\).

The numerator of the fraction in QWK is the mean squared difference between the two scores, \(\sum_{i,k} (i-k)^2 p_{ik} = E[(X - Y)^2]\). The denominator is the same expectation for two independent raters with the same margins. Expanding the square, \[E[(X-Y)^2] = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\sigma_{XY} + (\mu_X - \mu_Y)^2,\] and under independence the covariance term is 0. So \[\text{QWK} = 1 - \frac{\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\sigma_{XY} + (\mu_X - \mu_Y)^2}{\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 + (\mu_X - \mu_Y)^2} = \frac{2\sigma_{XY}}{\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 + (\mu_X - \mu_Y)^2}.\] This is exact, and it is Lin’s (1989) concordance correlation coefficient. Three things follow. With equal means and variances, QWK is the Pearson correlation. A difference in means lowers it (the widget’s shift), and so does a difference in spread even when the correlation is 1 (the widget’s compression). And Fleiss and Cohen (1973) showed that weighted kappa with these weights is, apart from terms of order \(1/n\), the intraclass correlation that counts rater differences as error: the absolute-agreement coefficient of Many sources of error, if you’ve done it.

Can the text of an item predict its difficulty?

Recall from What is an item?: the LLTM writes each item’s easiness as a weighted sum of its features, and an item residual with SD \(\sigma_\varepsilon\) gives a size to what the features leave out. For the verbal aggression items, features coded from the design explained 89% of the variance in easiness, and the residual SD was 0.34.

Where do the features come from? Some are counts from the text, such as word count and sentence length, the stuff of readability measures like the Lexile framework (Stenner, Burdick, Sanford & Burdick, 2006). Some are coded by experts from a model of how items are solved: for paragraph-comprehension items from the GRE, variables describing the decision among options accounted for difficulty and those describing the text did not (Gorin & Embretson, 2006). And some are representations of the text from a language model (Benedetto et al., 2023, survey the methods). Kapoor, Truong, Haber, Ruiz-Primo and Domingue (2025) predicted the difficulty of state reading items from annotated linguistic, test and context features: a correlation of 0.77 with the true difficulties, and a root mean squared error of 0.59 against 0.92 for predicting the mean. Embeddings from language models improved the prediction only marginally, and either kind of feature alone did about as well.

Is a correlation of 0.77 good? Two yardsticks assume different uses. Accuracy (a correlation, an RMSE) assumes the prediction is the product, as when sorting a bank. Respondents assumes the point is to calibrate items with fewer people. A calibration on \(n\) respondents estimates \(b\) with a standard error of about \(1/\sqrt{n\,E[p(1-p)]}\), since each respondent adds \(p(1-p)\) to the information about the item’s location (the curvature of The likelihood and logistic regression). A prediction whose errors have SD \(\sigma\) is therefore as precise as a calibration on \[n^* = \frac{1}{\sigma^2\,E[p(1-p)]}\] respondents.

I use the second yardstick. The prize in predicting difficulty is skipping or shrinking the field test, so I want to know how many respondents a prediction is worth; accuracy statistics don’t say.

Language models as simulated respondents

Argyle et al. (2023) conditioned GPT-3 on the backgrounds of real survey participants and found that it reproduced the response patterns of many subgroups. For items, Liu et al. (2025) had six models answer college algebra items: each model’s abilities were narrowly spread, an ensemble of models came closer to students, and the item parameters correlated above 0.8 with those from students for GPT-3.5. Zhou, Liu, Tang and Wilson (2026), a preprint, address that narrow spread by telling the model which student to be: each prompt describes a profile of fraction-subtraction skills from a cognitive diagnosis model. With profiles, the models’ scores spread more like students’, and the item difficulties from their answers came closer to the students’ (in the strongest case, a rank correlation of about 0.9 against 0.24 without profiles; see Latent classes and cognitive diagnosis). Scarlatos, Fernandez, Ormerod, Lottridge and Lan (2025) train a model so that simulated students at an instructed ability answer as an item response model says they should, and use the simulated answers to predict the difficulty of open-ended items.

Does simulation beat the simpler route of the last section? A simulator is a difficulty predictor, so it answers to the same yardstick, and it has to get each item right: a model may find an item easy or hard for reasons of its own.

Automatic item generation, and checking what it makes

Items have been generated by rule for decades. A cognitive design system builds in the features that make an item hard and calibrates the design principles rather than each item (Embretson, 1999); item models are templates with slots filled by rule (Gierl & Haladyna, 2012; for medical multiple-choice items, Gierl, Lai & Turner, 2012). Language models write items without a template. AI-GENIE generates a pool with a language model and prunes it with network psychometrics before anyone answers (Russell-Lasalandra, Christensen & Golino, 2024).

Whoever writes the items, the questions are the same: does each measure its construct, how hard is it, do the wrong options draw anyone? Distractors that draw almost nobody are common in human-written tests too: Haladyna and Downing (1993) found that items seldom had more than three options that performed acceptably, and a meta-analysis of 80 years of studies supports three options (Rodriguez, 2005).

Impossible questions: does the text predict difficulty?

The Forecasting Proficiency Test (Himmelstein et al., 2025; materials and data at forecastingresearch/fpt) includes an impossible-question task adapted from Bennett and Steyvers (2022). Each question offers two answers and an opt-out. Most have a right answer; some ask about things that don’t exist, and the right response is to opt out. Two examples from the study’s data file are “Not all metals are attracted to magnets” (true or false) and “What’s the habitat of a Rojixon bird?” (deserts or tropical rainforests). Keying: 1 is the keyed answer, or an opt-out on an impossible question. A gentle note from the IRW’s item-text notes: the instructions said opt-outs weren’t scored, but on answerable questions they count as wrong.

# Two text features, as in the IRW vignette on item language and difficulty: word
# count, and mean word length in characters with punctuation removed.
text_features <- function(txt) {
  words <- strsplit(trimws(gsub("[[:punct:]]", " ", txt)), "\\s+")
  words <- lapply(words, function(w) w[nchar(w) > 0])
  data.frame(word_count = lengths(strsplit(trimws(txt), "\\s+")),
             mean_word_length = vapply(words, function(w) if (length(w)) mean(nchar(w)) else NA_real_, 0))
}
items <- cbind(items, text_features(items$question_text))
items$impossible <- as.integer(items$question_type == "IQ")
# Proportion correct among those who answered, and its logit. (An opt-out is scored
# correct on an impossible question and wrong on an answerable one.)
items$p <- as.vector(tapply(iq$resp, iq$item, mean)[items$item])
items$n <- as.vector(table(iq$item)[items$item])
items$logit_p <- qlogis((items$p * items$n + 0.5) / (items$n + 1))

Code 
# What is each prediction worth, in respondents? One respondent carries E[p(1 - p)]
# units of information about an item's location (Rasch model, theta ~ N(0, sd_theta));
# a prediction with error SD sigma is as good as a calibration on
# n* = 1 / (sigma^2 * E[p(1 - p)]) respondents. E[p(1 - p)] is averaged over items at
# the locations each model predicts.
epq <- function(loc, sd_theta) integrate(function(t)
  plogis(loc + t) * (1 - plogis(loc + t)) * dnorm(t, 0, sd_theta), -Inf, Inf)$value
worth <- function(m) {
  v <- as.data.frame(VarCorr(m)); sigma <- v$sdcor[v$grp == "item"]; sd_theta <- v$sdcor[v$grp == "id"]
  loc <- unique(model.matrix(m) %*% fixef(m))
  info <- mean(sapply(loc, epq, sd_theta = sd_theta))
  c(sigma = sigma, info_per_respondent = info, respondents_worth = 1 / (sigma^2 * info))
}

  2. Without the impossible questions. Drop the 18 impossible questions and refit the LLTM with word count and mean word length. What do they explain, and what is the prediction worth? Code a feature of your own from the text (true–false or not, say) and test it.

Across the IRW

Can item text predict difficulty across the IRW? compute.R takes each English dichotomous cognitive table with at least 200 respondents and item text in the IRW, and correlates word count and mean word length with the logit of proportion correct within the table. Pooling items across tables would confound an item’s length with the test it came from. No feature from a language model is used yet.

Recall from Constructs and construct maps: of the nine number-series items, NS_2 was solved by 84% of those who answered it and NS_6, whose key accepts 165 where tripling the steps gives 120, by 1.5%. The corpus run includes that table to check its own pipeline.
